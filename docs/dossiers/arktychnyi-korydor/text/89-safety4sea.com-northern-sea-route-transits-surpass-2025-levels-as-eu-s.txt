Northern Sea Route transits surpass 2025 levels as EU seeks greater Arctic access
by
The Editorial Team
October 20, 2026
in
Arctic
Above image is used for illustration purposes only
Facebook
Twitter
Email
Linkedin
Commercial transits through Russia’s Northern Sea Route (NSR) have exceeded the full-year 2025 total, with increased tanker, bulk carrier and container ship activity highlighting growing use of the Arctic corridor.
A
ccording to the Centre for High North Logistics (CHNL), 104 confirmed transit voyages were recorded along the Northern Sea Route by 30 September 2026, exceeding the 103 voyages registered during the entire 2025 navigation season. Estimated cargo volumes reached 3.7 million tonnes, while the combined deadweight of vessels increased by 26% to 7.8 million DWT.

The figures come as the European Union seeks to maintain international access to Arctic shipping routes
and strengthen its focus on maritime security, surveillance and infrastructure protection in the region.
Tankers and bulk carriers contribute to traffic growth
According to CHNL’s analysis of AIS data from MarineTraffic by Kpler, oil tankers accounted for a substantial share of NSR cargo movements during the 2026 navigation season.
The data identified 22 eastbound tanker voyages carrying an estimated 2.1 million tonnes, primarily crude oil. Bulk carriers also contributed to transit activity, with 13 eastbound cargo voyages and 27 westbound voyages recorded by the end of September.
Container shipping activity increased, with 13 transits recorded during the same period.
These included four international Asia–Europe container voyages
without Russian endpoints, compared with one during the entire 2025 season.
CHNL indicated that the total number of transits could reach approximately 150 by the end of 2026, although this remains a projection rather than a confirmed outcome.
The latest figures provide further evidence of increasing commercial interest in the NSR, while its wider role in international container shipping remains subject to seasonal and operational constraints.
The development follows growing interest in China–Europe Arctic container services,
previously examined in SAFETY4SEA’s analysis of the route’s transition from trial voyages to scheduled operations.
EU focuses on Arctic maritime access and security
The increase in Arctic shipping activity coincides with renewed EU attention to maritime access and security in the region. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas has called for cooperation with the United States and Canada to maintain international access to Arctic shipping routes.
The EU’s Arctic policy discussions have also placed emphasis on maritime surveillance, monitoring of shipping activity, protection of critical infrastructure and icebreaker capabilities.
These priorities reflect the growing strategic importance of Arctic waters for commercial transportation and maritime security.
However, the EU’s stated objective of maintaining access should not be interpreted as an established change to the legal or administrative arrangements governing navigation along Russia’s Northern Sea Route.
The latest traffic figures indicate increasing use of the NSR by several vessel segments, particularly tankers and bulk carriers.
Nevertheless, the route remains subject to seasonal ice conditions and operational restrictions that influence vessel suitability and voyage planning.
The expansion of traffic therefore does not necessarily indicate that the NSR has become a year-round alternative to established international shipping corridors.
