# Hundreds of shadow fleet ships sail under a false flag. The West is coming for them – but is it fast enough?

More than 500 shadow fleet tankers are sailing without a valid flag while transporting oil from Russia, Iran, and Venezuela, an investigation has found. International maritime law allows stateless ships to be intercepted at sea, as demonstrated recently by the U.S. and France. Although European nations have pledged tougher enforcement, analysts warn that they are lagging behind the shadow fleet.

### Print settings

For shadow fleet vessels transporting oil from Iran, Russia and Venezuela in violation of international sanctions, sailing under fake flags has been a key strategy to avoid scrutiny.

Yet recent high-profile seizures of stateless oil tankers by the United States and France suggest that this tactic could now backfire.

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), ships operating without a valid nationality can be boarded by any state.

An investigation by *FTM* and media partners calculated that about a third of the shadow fleet use false flags, leaving more than 500 vessels vulnerable to being intercepted at sea.

Analysts told *Follow the Money *that a new front may be opening in the West’s fight against such ships, as authorities seek to use the full force of international maritime law.

Nele Matz-Lück, professor of international law at Kiel University, said the U.S. and French crackdowns showed that countries are beginning to interpret the regulations more broadly – and be proactive rather than “just stand by and watch”.

“I think it is a consequence of the frustration of recent months and years, of having so little authority and wanting to take more robust action,” she told *FTM.*

Yet some maritime and legal experts warn that Europe is moving too slowly in the cat-and-mouse game with the shadow fleet.

**Rough passage**

International law stipulates that all vessels must be registered to a country and fly its flag (see box).

However, many sanctioned oil tankers circumvent this requirement by buying fraudulent flags from companies that falsely claim to operate a nation’s ship registry – including for jurisdictions that don’t even maintain one – a previous *FTM* [investigation](https://www.ftm.eu/articles/sanctioned-tankers-buy-fake-flags-sint-maarten?share=YZMGOE1Fvqw0Hv3uZANHiglwFcB7mlT5%2Bk7ZmBhQlv90Qxf4MIKIQGUCkn4CR8w%3D) revealed. 

Until recently, the West’s response was largely limited to notifying the International Maritime Organization (IMO) and appealing to states that issue flags to cancel false registrations.

“One question is: how willing are European countries to let the problem escalate?”


But the United States and France have raised the stakes recently.

In late January, the French navy [intercepted](https://x.com/EmmanuelMacron/status/2014341199507706242) a Russian oil tanker – the Grinch – in the Mediterranean on suspicion of sailing under a false flag and being part of the shadow fleet. 

Several of France’s allies, including the United Kingdom, supported the operation.

This came amid a flurry of action by the United States, which has seized seven Venezuela-linked oil tankers since mid-December as part of U.S. President Donald Trump’s plan to take control of the country’s energy resources.

Four of the ships were flying false flags from Panama, Guyana and Timor-Leste, according to the IMO database.

In the most striking case, the Bella 1 – an oil tanker falsely flying a Guyana flag and bound for Venezuela – was pursued by U.S. authorities in the Caribbean Sea in mid-December.

The ship then altered course, changed its name to Marinera and registered with Russia. Its crew even painted the Russian flag on the hull, according to [media reports](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/30/us/politics/oil-tanker-venezuela-us-russia-bella-1.html).

Despite this attempt to evade capture, it was apprehended by U.S. forces on 7 January in the Atlantic Ocean near Iceland.

All of these tankers are part of the shadow fleet – calculated at more than 1,300 vessels – used to circumvent Western sanctions on oil shipments from Iran, Russia and Venezuela.

At least a third of the fleet – over 500 ships – fly under a false flag, the investigation found.

Flag states are responsible for ensuring that vessels registered under their flag comply with international rules – from sanctions enforcement to crew protections and safety regulations.

Shadow fleet tankers often exploit registries in countries with weaker oversight, such as Sierra Leone or Cameroon, where enforcement of these standards is less stringent.

But the number of flag states is shrinking.

Under growing pressure from Europe and the United States, several countries have begun tightening oversight and removing suspect vessels from their registries.

This is reflected in the responses that *FTM* received from a number of these nations.

For example, although Sierra Leone did not disclose how many ships are currently listed on its registry, its government told* FTM* that it will remove all vessels subject to sanctions. 

The West African nation is one of the shadow fleet’s most frequently used flags, with 85 tankers registered to Sierra Leone currently sanctioned by the EU.

The tiny Pacific Island nation of Palau has been even more proactive.

Authorities told *FTM *they had already removed 29 of 43 identified shadow fleet vessels from the nation’s registry.

## Europe on high alert

Most false flag tankers operate in the Gulf region, Asia and Venezuela, according to analysis of shipping data from the IMO, Equasis, Global Fishing Watch and Marine Traffic.

However, *FTM *and its media partners calculated that of these nearly 500 ships, 61 sailed through European waters between 1 November 2025 and 5 February 2026. 

Twenty passed through the English Channel – one of the busiest maritime routes in Europe – and the North Sea.

This highlights how, despite years of efforts to rein in the shadow fleet, such tankers continue to operate within sight of major maritime and security authorities.

For example, the [Elyte](https://www.opensanctions.org/entities/NK-UN2ZMUxjk6ZYNP5bqyUUxe/) – which sails under a false Sierra Leonean flag – crossed the Dutch North Sea on 8 and 9 January fully laden with Russian crude.

The [Starlit](https://www.opensanctions.org/entities/NK-cB9crkT8mzoLTP6hpnSUbr/) – also operating under a fake flag from the West African nation – followed the same route on 24 January carrying Russian oil.

Both ships have been sanctioned by the European Union and the United Kingdom.

 Another Russian shadow fleet vessel, named [Peace](https://war-sanctions.gur.gov.ua/en/transport/shadow-fleet/583),** **last month bypassed the North Sea altogether by sailing around Ireland and Scotland. 

This is an increasingly common detour for tankers that appear to be seeking to avoid inspections in the English Channel.

After years of efforts to curb Russia’s illicit oil trade, European countries in recent months have issued increasingly blunt warnings regarding tankers that operate without a valid flag

In October, all nations bordering the Baltic Sea and the North Sea signed a declaration stating that a ship without nationality “is not entitled to innocent passage, transit passage, or freedom of navigation”.

“On the high seas or in the exclusive economic zone (EEZ), a State may exercise the right of visit if there are reasonable grounds to suspect that a ship is without nationality,” it read.

On 15 December 2025, the EU confirmed this right in a [statement](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-16829-2025-INIT/en/pdf) citing the international agreed laws of the sea under UNCLOS.

This appears to pave the way for tougher action against shadow fleet tankers suspected of flying false flags.

But that will depend on the resolve of Europe’s authorities, according to Yf Reykers, associate professor of international relations at Maastricht University.

“One question is: how willing are European countries to let the problem escalate?” he told *FTM. *

“We are seeing Russia becoming more assertive, sometimes sending armed men on their ships and deploying drones. European countries differ in their responses to this.”

**Cat-and-mouse chase**

There is also the question of urgency.

European nations are too hesitant, said Niels Woudstra, a former Dutch naval officer and expert in international maritime law.

While discussions among policymakers are ongoing, and several EU countries say they are working on new legislation, shadow fleet vessels are already adapting their tactics to stay one step ahead.

Over the last week, 20 tankers ditched their false flags and registered with Cameroon’s obscure but legitimate registry, IMO data shows.

Many others – at least 17 in recent weeks – have followed the example of the Marinera, leaving fraudulent registries and [joining the Russian flag](https://www.lloydslist.com/LL1155992/Venezuela-risk-accelerates-Russian-intake-of-shadow-fleet-vessels). 

Ultimately, a stricter interpretation of international maritime law might also have consequences for Western ships, warned Matz-Lück of Kiel University.

“We just need to be aware that other countries will then do the same,” she said.

“So it is not a right that only we [Europe] can claim for ourselves, but a right that countries such as China – as a major player and large nation – and other countries will interpret in exactly the same way.”
