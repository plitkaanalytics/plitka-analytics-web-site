Foreword

          Welcome to the fifth edition of our Cyber Trends and Insights in the Marine Environment
          (CTIME) report. As we present our overall cyber findings from calendar year 2025, this
          report arrives at a significant inflection point for the Coast Guard. Driven by the largest
          capital investment in our history, we are actively strengthening Coast Guard Cyber
          Command (CGCYBER) to combat the evolving threats in the cyber domain. Coast Guard
          personnel across the Service are aggressively executing transformational changes. Our
          desired outcomes for the Nation are clear, the Coast Guard will: save lives; control, secure,
          and defend our border and maritime approaches; facilitate commerce vital to economic
          prosperity; and respond to any crisis or contingency. In our contested maritime environment,
          the cyber domain is a critical front.

          In 2025, we deployed cyber teams as part of Maritime Interdiction Operations targeting
          Dark Fleet Vessels. Also, for the first time, our teams assessed partners who integrated
          artificial intelligence into their cyber defenses, yielding valuable lessons on the importance
          of proper configuration. We also examined the security of Terminal Operating Systems,
          which are essential to the flow of commerce. However, even as we innovate, adversaries
          continue to rely on fundamental attack vectors. Reported incidents were up 17% in 2025,
          and phishing remains a primary method for initial access. Despite the rise in reported
          incidents, operational impacts were thankfully limited. Our findings continue to underscore
          the need for a balanced approach to protecting the Marine Transportation System (MTS)
          which emphasizes not only mastering cybersecurity fundamentals, but also building
          resilience to ensure continuity of operations.

          President Trump’s Cyber Strategy for America highlights securing U.S. critical infrastructure
          as one of six Pillars of Action. Securing our nation’s MTS is a vital part of this effort.
          Confronting the dynamic cyber threats we face requires a collective, unified effort. While
          CGCYBER is dedicated to applying the Federal government’s cyber capabilities to protect
          the MTS, our greatest strength lies in our people and our partnerships. The collaborative
          work between our exceptional workforce and our partners in the public and private sectors
          is the true foundation of our ability to secure our ports and waterways against any threat.
          Cybersecurity is a team sport, and this annual report would not be possible without
          partnerships. Thank you for collaborating with us, and we hope you enjoy this year’s edition!

          Semper Paratus,

          RDML Jason Tama
          Rear Admiral, United States Coast Guard
          Commander, Coast Guard Cyber Command

What’s New in 2025?

▪ In 2025, for the first time, CGCYBER CPTs worked with partners who integrated artificial intelligence (AI) into their

  cybersecurity defense. The results were mixed: in one case, AI immediately detected and stopped our penetration
  testers’ every move, while in other cases, AI failed to detect any of our teams’ actions. The success of these tools
  is largely tied to how the system is configured and tuned.

▪ The demand for CGCYBER missions on operational technology (OT) systems continued to grow. We prioritized

  maturing and expanding OT system mission capabilities. In 2025, 62% of missions included OT in the scope,
  an increase from 46% in 2024.

▪ In 2025, CGCYBER modified how we categorize findings from our threat hunt missions. Our new system helps

  us better articulate the types of malicious findings we observe on mission and are discussed further in the
  Hunt and Incident Response section (page 13).

▪ As a member of the Cyber Mission Force, CGCYBER expanded its role in the Joint Force, executing multiple

  forward deployed operations ranging from threat hunt missions with United States Cyber Command
  (USCYBER) to Maritime Interdiction Operations of Dark Fleet Vessels with traditional assault and law
  enforcement forces, all in support of achieving U.S. strategic objectives.

▪ In 2025, CGCYBER’s first National Mission Team (NMT) reached Full Operating Capability and was established

  as a new Command. 1915 NMT represents the Coast Guard as an embedded unit of the Cyber National
  Mission Force under USCYBER.

▪ The Coast Guard received the largest capital investment by the American people in our history. On July 4,

  2025, after passage by Congress, the President signed into law $24.5 billion in appropriations to recapitalize
  aircraft, cutters, boats, shore infrastructure, and technology.

▪ Another key tenet of the service’s transformational change focuses on strengthening CGCYBER. The Coast

  Guard will grow and transform CGCYBER to ensure the ability to address current and future threats in the
  cyber domain, and to improve interoperability with the Joint Force.

▪ On January 17, 2025, the Coast Guard issued a Final Rule4 related to Cybersecurity in the MTS. On July

  16, 2025, the new regulations applicable to Maritime Transportation System Security Act (MTSA)-regulated
  entities went into effect starting with mandatory reporting for cyber incidents. By July 16, 2027, the first
  annual Cybersecurity Assessment must be conducted, and a Cybersecurity Plan must be submitted to Coast
  Guard for approval. It is important to note that even with issuance of the new regulations, CGCYER Cyber
  Protection Team operations remain non-regulatory activities.

▪ Finally, the Coast Guard stood up a new Maritime Cybersecurity Policy office. See the last section of this report

  for insights from its new office chief, Charles “Nicholas” Parham in “Charting the Next Course: Cybersecurity
  Governance in a Contested Maritime Domain” (page 23).

Mission Overview

CGCYBER CPTs conduct non-regulatory activities to prevent, detect, and respond to cyber
threats impacting critical infrastructure in the MTS.

The teams deploy and operate upon requests from partners in support of Coast Guard Operational Commanders
and collaborate with public and private organizations globally.

In 2025, CGCYBER completed 42 missions with industry partners. This report is based on the data and analysis
collected during these missions, cross-referenced with incidents reported to the CGCYBER MCRB, to provide a
comprehensive understanding of current and emerging cyber threats. Figure 1 provides visual representations of

Conclusion
Recommended Best Practices
A list of recommended best practices to harden the TOS and its surrounding network
are below. These closely follow the top recommended mitigations from the 2024
CTIME9 report and address the common vulnerabilities mentioned previously:

■ Restrict Public-Facing Access.
   □ Remove any public-facing login portals intended for internal use.
   □ Place these services behind internal firewalls and require access through
       secure, controlled channels like a VPN.

■ Strengthen Authentication Policies.
   □ Ensure password policies are aligned with NIST SP 800-63B-4,10 as
       amended, guidance. This includes complexity requirements, lockout
       thresholds, and MFA implementation to mitigate password-guessing and
       brute-force attacks.

■ Implement Network Segmentation.
   □ Establish clear segmentation between the IT network and the OT network
       connected to TOS services.
   □ Follow best practices from the 2023 CTIME Report,11 including:

      ▪ Use of Demilitarized Zones (DMZs) between IT and OT zones.
      ▪ Audit and strictly control all inter-zone communications to prevent

           lateral movement.

      ▪ Apply the Principle of Least Privilege to all accounts and services.
      ▪ Limit OT network access to essential personnel and systems only.

■ Vulnerability Management.
   □ Conduct routine vulnerability scanning and ensure timely patching of
       systems and applications.
   □ For systems that are End-Of-Life and cannot be immediately upgraded,
       ensure compensating controls such as network isolation and allow listing
       are in place and documented.

■ Secure Data in Transit and at Rest.
   □ Use modern, secure protocols for all data transmission. Examples include TLS
       v1.2 or higher, SFTP, and SSH.
   □ Encrypt all sensitive data stored within TOS databases and file servers to
       protect data at rest.

Cyber Risks to Dark Fleet Vessels
In the physical domain, Dark Fleet Vessels operate in the shadows to avoid sanctions, skirting inspections
and international standards that ensure the safe operations of commercial vessels, making them potentially
unseaworthy, and especially hazardous when laden with cargo. Likewise, Dark Fleet Vessels are neither
obedient to Flag or Port State rules and regulations nor subject to due diligence by corporations to secure
IT and OT networks aboard. Often, Dark Fleet Vessels have modernized high-bandwidth commercial satellite
communications systems supporting IT networks that, if exploited, could put the safety of the vessel and the
Coast Guard or Department of War forces at risk. Further, they regularly use pirated software littered with
varying types of malware which put their systems at risk.

Cyber Control Teams
Once deployed onboard a Dark Fleet vessel, a Cyber Control Team works to quickly gain Positive Control
(POSCON) of the cyber terrain for the ship. Time permitting, the Cyber Control Team can conduct further analysis
and defensive efforts. These activities are conducted for the safe navigation of the vessel and the protection of
the law enforcement boarding team and ship’s crew.

Appendix A

Maritime Cyber Information Products

CGCYBER MCRB generates two types of information products. These products are Maritime Cyber Alerts (MCA)
and Maritime Cyber Bulletins (MCB). Both products are TLP:CLEAR13 and may be shared without restriction. MCAs
provide specific and actionable mitigating measures for critical vulnerabilities or threat group activity. MCBs provide
awareness on cyber trends and threats MCRB has observed in the Marine Transportation System (MTS) along with
general recommendations and resources to assist MTS entities in hardening cybersecurity posture.

Maritime Cyber Alerts Released in 2025

▪ 01-25 Exploitation of Vulnerabilities in Ivanti Cloud Services Appliance (CSA)14

  This alert provides information on the known exploitation of Common Vulnerability and Exposures (CVEs) for
  Ivanti Cloud Services Appliance (CSA). It identifies targeted applications and systems, threat actor tactics,
  mitigations, and the known indicators of compromise (IOCs).

▪ 02-25 Ghost (Cring) Ransomware Targeting Critical Infrastructure15

  This alert highlights the Ghost Ransomware group conducting malicious activity within critical infrastructure. It
  identifies commonly targeted systems, tactics, mitigations, and known IOCs.

▪ 04-25 Scattered Spider Targeting Critical Infrastructure16

  This alert highlights the Scattered Spider threat actor targeting MTS entities with sophisticated phishing
  techniques to gain access to targeted companies’ systems. It identifies threat actor tactics, mitigation
  measures, and known IOCs.

Maritime Cyber Bulletins Released in 2025

▪ 01-25 Updated Incident Management Handbook17

  This bulletin highlights the U.S. Coast Guard’s release of an updated Incident Management Handbook to include the
  addition of a cyber incident section. It identifies recommendations and resources to help response to MTS incidents.

▪ 02-25 Trojanized SonicWall VPN Client18

  This bulletin provides information on threat actors targeting the MTS through distribution of trojanized versions
  of VPNs to steal credentials from remote users. It identifies recommendations and resources to mitigate the
  threat to MTS partners.
