    Overview

    We assess that expanding infrastructure development at Libyan air bases where Africa Corps has an established presence based on open-source reporting likely advances Russia's military foothold and enables operations across North Africa and the Sahel, while maintaining a military presence along NATO's southern flank.

    This project analyzes satellite imagery and open-source intelligence (OSINT) to examine improvements at six existing Libyan air bases since the arrival of Africa Corps, a Russian state-controlled military group, in April 2024.

    Activity

    The Libyan air bases covered in this report include the following: 1) Al-Khadim, 2) Al-Jufra, 3) Tamanhint (also known as Sabha), 4) Matan al-Sarra, 5) Al-Qardabiyah, and 6) Brak al-Shati. Although we have not found open-source reporting of Africa Corps' presence at other civilian airports or air bases in Libya, varying degrees of construction are visible at eight out of 21 additional airports throughout Libya, so Africa Corps activity is still possible at these locations. Open-source reporting also indicates Russian plans to invest in Tobruk Port and Tobruk Air Base, though construction activity is not visible at these sites on satellite imagery. Russia likely provides the planning, funding, construction, and personnel for expansion at these air bases, which likely secures operational access for Africa Corps in Libya. However, in the case of Matan Al-Sarra, Libyan personnel likely led implementation while Russian and Syrian elements supported. Direct reporting does not tie the Kremlin to funding every instance of expansion, and independent initiatives by the Libyan government cannot be ruled out.

    Methodology

    We used OSINT and satellite imagery to assess Africa Corps activity at Libyan air bases. To identify air base locations and gather information on Africa Corps activity in Libya, we relied on publicly available think tank and news reporting and social media posts. We utilized commercial imagery to visualize buildup at Libyan air bases since 2024, identify new infrastructure, and corroborate OSINT reporting on construction activities.

    Conclusion

    Since April 2024, six air bases with reported Africa Corps presence (Al-Khadim, Al-Jufra, Tamanhint, Matan al-Sarra, Al-Qardabiyah, and Brak al-Shati) experienced new construction and renovations of buildings, hangars, barracks, aprons, and runways. Imagery analysis of 21 additional Libyan airports found new construction at eight air bases, though we were unable to confirm Africa Corps activity at any of them, and many are working to repair damage from conflicts in prior years. A Russian foothold at Libyan air bases creates a launch point for shipments and operations in the North Africa and Sahel region, especially as Russian access to key bases in Syria are in question.Talbot, Frank.       Look Ahead

      Continued infrastructure developments at Libyan air bases, airports, and maritime facilities will provide key indicators of the scale and trajectory of Africa Corps expansion in Libya. Particular attention should be paid to future activity at the Tobruk Air Base and Port, and the expansion and continued use of Libyan air bases in Russian operations across Africa.

      Things to Watch

      • Continue monitoring infrastructure developments at Libyan air bases with an established Africa Corps presence.
      • Monitor infrastructure developments at other Libyan airports as a possible indicator of future Africa Corps presence.
      • Monitor any new construction at Tobruk Port and Tobruk Air Base.
      • Track flights to see whether Russia continues to use Libyan air bases as part of operational air bridges in support of various conflicts in Africa.
      • Look for infrastructure development and the docking of Russian ships at Tobruk Naval Base, possibly under the cover of Belarusian activity, as an indicator of Russia’s growing air and naval military presence in Libya.
