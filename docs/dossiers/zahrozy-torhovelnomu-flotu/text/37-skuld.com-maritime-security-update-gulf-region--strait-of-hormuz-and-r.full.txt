Maritime security update: Gulf Region / Strait of Hormuz and Red Sea
Port news
Published: 2 March 2026
Updated:
17 August 2026
Image credit to: Visuals6x / Shutterstock.com
Update 17 August 2026
Almost two months after the United States and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding aimed at reducing hostilities, the maritime security environment across the Gulf region remains characterised by elevated threat levels, reduced traffic volumes and continued operational uncertainty.
While the Strait of Hormuz remains open to commercial traffic, vessel movements remain below historical levels and operators continue to face a combination of security threats, navigation hazards and operational uncertainty. Recent weeks have seen continued Iranian surveillance, vessel hailing and enforcement-related activity in and around the Strait of Hormuz, together with a number of reported missile, UAV and other maritime security incidents affecting regional shipping. Navigation hazards also remain a concern, including continued GNSS interference, AIS anomalies and the risk posed by drifting or uncharted mines. In the Red Sea, recent Houthi messaging has indicated an expanded focus on Saudi-linked shipping and a broader area of concern extending beyond the Bab el-Mandeb into parts of the northern Red Sea. At the same time, intelligence reporting has pointed to a renewed increase in Somali piracy activity affecting vessels operating near the Somali coast and the Gulf of Aden.
Strait of Hormuz: open, but operating under elevated risk
Recent weeks have demonstrated that the principal concern for maritime operators is no longer whether the Strait of Hormuz will close entirely, but rather the continued uncertainty surrounding safe and predictable vessel movements through one of the world's most important maritime chokepoints.
Commercial traffic continues to transit the Strait, and the southern route remains available. However, multiple industry and military advisories report significantly reduced traffic volumes compared with normal operating conditions, reflecting continued caution among operators and charterers.
The operating environment continues to be characterised by heightened naval activity, competing transit narratives, ongoing monitoring of merchant vessels and a sustained presence of military assets throughout the area. Recent advisories continue to assess the threat level in the Strait of Hormuz as severe.
Transit arrangements and freedom of navigation
A further development since late June has been the continued debate over transit arrangements in the Strait of Hormuz.
Industry reporting indicates ongoing efforts by Iranian authorities to influence routing procedures and vessel movements through the Strait. At the same time, international maritime organisations and naval stakeholders have maintained that commercial shipping continues to enjoy freedom of navigation through available transit routes and that no single authority controls passage through the Strait.
Recent advisories have reiterated that vessels may continue to use the southern route through the Strait and that commercial transits remain possible, although operators should expect increased naval interaction, VHF hailing, surveillance activity and requests for information. Mariners are also encouraged to maintain close engagement with UKMTO, NCAGS, and other recognised reporting mechanisms to support situational awareness and voyage planning.
The consequence is an operating environment in which commercial navigation remains possible, but where routing decisions, security considerations and risk management continue to require significantly greater attention than under normal trading conditions.
Continued attacks, surveillance and enforcement activity
Despite a reduction in large-scale military exchanges, maritime security incidents continue to directly affect commercial shipping and crew safety.
Recent reporting highlights projectile strikes, suspected UAV attacks, missile incidents, vessel monitoring and harassment activity involving merchant shipping operating in and around the Strait of Hormuz, the Gulf of Oman and Bab el-Mandeb. The
IMO’s latest incident reporting
records 65 confirmed incidents and 17 confirmed seafarer fatalities in the Strait of Hormuz and the wider Middle East, as at 11 August 2026. The same reporting includes incidents involving damaged vessels, abandoned vessels, injured crew and missing seafarers.
Casualties have also been reported in the Red Sea. On 11 August, the small cargo vessel
Tihamah
was reportedly struck in the Bab el-Mandeb area. Lloyd’s List initially reported four seafarers feared dead, while Reuters later reported that four crew members and two Yemeni rescuers were killed, with further injuries reported among crew and rescue personnel.
In the Gulf of Oman, the container vessel
Vela Nova
was reportedly struck on 11 August and left not under command, although all 17 crew members were accounted for according to Lloyd’s List reporting.
These incidents underline that the current threat environment is not limited to disruption, delay or routing uncertainty. It continues to involve direct risk to vessels and seafarers. Iranian surveillance activity, including UAV overflights, VHF hailing and vessel monitoring, also continues to be reported, while international naval forces remain engaged in monitoring, verification and enforcement activities in the wider region.
Navigation hazards remain a significant concern
Alongside the direct security threat, mariners continue to face a range of navigational challenges.
Persistent reports of GNSS interference, AIS anomalies and electronic disruption continue across the Gulf region and adjoining waters. Advisories also continue to reference the risk posed by drifting or uncharted mines in and around the traditional traffic separation scheme, while mine clearance and surveying operations remain ongoing.
As a result, operators should ensure that bridge teams remain prepared to verify positional information using alternative means when necessary and maintain vigilance throughout transit.
Red Sea: evolving Houthi posture
Developments in the Red Sea continue to warrant close monitoring.
Recent Houthi statements have suggested an expansion of the group's stated area of concern beyond the Bab el-Mandeb and southern Red Sea. Industry reporting indicates that messaging has increasingly focused on vessels associated with Saudi interests, and suggests that additional maritime access points could be contested.
At the same time, missile and drone capabilities assessed to be available to the Houthis remain in place, and threat assessments continue to note the potential for future disruption affecting regional shipping routes. Commercial traffic through the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb continues, but at reduced levels compared with historical norms.
Of particular relevance for operators trading to Saudi Arabian ports, recent reporting suggests that AIS-off operations at Yanbu berths, waiting areas and approaches remain elevated, reflecting the continued sensitivity of the regional security environment.
Renewed concerns in the Gulf of Aden and the Western Indian Ocean
Beyond developments in the Gulf region and Red Sea, operators should continue to monitor security risks in the wider Gulf of Aden and Western Indian Ocean.
Recent intelligence reporting points to a renewed increase in Somali piracy activity, including several successful hijackings involving locally trading vessels operating near the Somali coast and the Gulf of Aden. While activity remains significantly below the levels experienced during the peak of Somali piracy, recent incidents demonstrate that piracy remains a credible threat, particularly for vessels operating close to the Somali coastline and established small-craft operating areas.
The wider regional security picture continues to be influenced by a combination of piracy concerns, spillover from regional conflicts, and maritime security incidents affecting vessels transiting the Gulf of Aden. Recent
US Maritime Administration (MARAD) guidance (2026-010)
has also highlighted ongoing risks associated with piracy, armed robbery, kidnapping and ransom activity across the Gulf of Aden, Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean, reinforcing the importance of voyage-specific risk assessments and adherence to regional reporting procedures.
Naval operations, industry reporting mechanisms, and BMP Maritime Security measures continue to play an important role in mitigating risk, and operators should remain vigilant when planning and conducting voyages through the region.
The security environment across the Gulf region and Red Sea has evolved from the acute disruption experienced earlier in the year into a more prolonged period of elevated maritime risk.
While commercial shipping continues to operate throughout the region, threat levels remain heightened due to the combination of ongoing military tensions, navigation hazards, electronic interference, maritime security incidents and uncertainty regarding transit arrangements.
Shipowners and operators trading in the region should continue to conduct voyage-specific threat and risk assessments, liaise closely with their war risk insurers and monitor the latest guidance issued by UKMTO, JMIC, NAVCENT NCAGS, EUNAVFOR ASPIDES and other relevant authorities.
Update 29 June 2026
Recent developments indicate an interruption in what had been an early and tentative recovery phase in the Strait of Hormuz, with the
temporary suspension of the IMO evacuation framework
and renewed security incidents affecting commercial shipping.
Evacuation framework paused following vessel attack
The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has confirmed that its evacuation operation has been temporarily paused following an attack on a commercial vessel in the Gulf of Oman.
The decision was taken to reconfirm that the necessary safety guarantees remain in place for vessels awaiting evacuation and for those operating in the wider region. The vessel involved had recently transited the Strait but was not participating in the IMO framework.
Prior to the pause, the operation had enabled the evacuation of more than 100 vessels and approximately 2,400–2,500 seafarers, representing an initial easing of congestion following the earlier disruption of traffic through the Gulf.
The suspension highlights the continued challenge of maintaining a coordinated transit regime in conditions where security assurances remain uncertain.
Routing uncertainty and enforcement pressures
The pause has also affected the temporary routing arrangements established to support evacuation movements. Information shared with the industry confirms that both northern and southern transit corridors remain subject to operational uncertainty.
At the same time, Iranian authorities have reiterated that only routes approved by Iran should be used, warning that vessels deviating from those routes may face penalties or further action.
This has reinforced an already complex operating environment, where vessels are effectively navigating between overlapping and, at times, conflicting expectations. In practice, some operators have responded by delaying or reversing voyages, particularly when attempting to transit via southern or non-Iranian-aligned routes.
Continued incidents underline fragile conditions
Recent reporting indicates that the security environment remains volatile.
Incidents in recent days include vessels receiving coercive instructions to divert or await authorisation in Iranian waters, as well as a UAV strike on a tanker near the Strait, resulting in structural damage but no reported casualties.
These events coincide with renewed military activity and exchanges between the United States and Iran, further placing pressure on the interim political framework agreed earlier in the month.
The cumulative effect is that the Strait continues to operate as a contested environment, with both physical and enforcement-related risks remaining elevated.
Shipping recovery continues, but remains fragile
There had been early indications of recovery in vessel movements following the US–Iran agreement, with traffic levels increasing to their highest point since the escalation of hostilities earlier this year.
Despite the pause in the evacuation framework and the recent attack, vessel movements have not ceased. Operators continue to transit selectively, reflecting a shift towards risk-managed rather than coordinated movement through the Strait.
However, overall activity remains materially below pre-conflict norms, and the latest developments have reinforced a cautious approach among shipowners, with some vessels delaying or adjusting transit decisions in response to security concerns.
The recovery is therefore uneven and remains closely linked to operational confidence, which continues to be affected by evolving security and regulatory conditions.
Outlook
The current situation reflects a fragile and incomplete recovery phase rather than a stabilised operating environment.
The pause in the IMO evacuation framework, combined with continued incidents and unresolved routing expectations, highlights the extent to which:
coordinated mechanisms remain dependent on credible security guarantees
commercial operations are increasingly driven by individual risk assessments
transit conditions remain sensitive to rapidly evolving developments
While shipping activity has resumed to a degree, the Strait of Hormuz continues to operate under conditions of partial recovery, regulatory ambiguity and ongoing volatility.
Update 25 June 2026
Recent developments indicate that the Strait of Hormuz has entered a transitional phase characterised by controlled evacuation, fragmented routing regimes and increasing regulatory oversight, rather than a return to normal commercial transit.
While a political agreement between the United States and Iran has created a framework for reducing hostilities, the practical conditions for merchant shipping remain constrained and subject to competing authorities.
Strait of Hormuz: evacuation framework replaces ad hoc transit
A material shift has occurred with the implementation of an
IMO-coordinated evacuation plan
designed to facilitate the departure of vessels stranded in the Gulf. The operation covers approximately 11,000 seafarers and several hundred vessels, reflecting the scale of disruption over recent months.
Movements are conducted on a phased, controlled basis, with vessels assigned to transit groups and allocated specific departure windows. Ships are instructed not to proceed independently and must await direct instructions before commencing transit.
Two temporary corridors are in use:
a northern route through Iranian waters
a southern route through Omani and UAE waters
These routes operate outside the established traffic separation scheme, which remains unsafe due to mine risk and degraded navigation conditions.
This process is best understood as a managed evacuation mechanism rather than a reopening of the Strait to free commercial movement.
The IMO has also issued
operational guidance and FAQs
clarifying how the evacuation process is expected to function in practice. This emphasises that vessel movements will be centrally coordinated, with ships required to wait for specific instructions, remain within assigned transit groups and follow routing directions issued by the relevant coastal State. The guidance further highlights that transits may be halted at short notice for safety or security reasons, reinforcing that passage through the Strait at this stage is conditional and subject to real-time control rather than routine navigation.
Competing routing authorities and conflicting instructions
The evacuation framework is complicated by the absence of a unified control structure.
Iran has stated that only routes approved by its authorities are permitted, with the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) indicating that vessels using alternative corridors may be considered in violation and subject to enforcement action.
At the same time, the IMO-led framework includes a southern route coordinated with Oman and supported by broader international engagement.
The result is a dual routing environment, where vessels must navigate between:
multinational evacuation guidance
Iranian routing and approval requirements
This divergence introduces operational uncertainty and creates potential exposure where compliance with one framework may conflict with the expectations of another.
Formalisation of transit conditions: insurance and route compliance
Alongside route control, Iran has introduced mandatory insurance requirements for vessels transiting the Strait.
Shipowners are required to obtain insurance approved by Iranian authorities, initially provided free of charge for a defined period but with provisions for future fees.
Transit is also linked to:
use of designated routes
adherence to Iranian operational requirements
Deviation from authorised routing is described by the IRGC as a violation and may trigger enforcement measures.
These developments mark a shift towards a structured transit regime, with regulatory and financial dimensions that shipowners and operators must consider when assessing voyages involving the Strait.
Political framework: limited operational clarity from the agreement
The
Memorandum of Understanding between the US and Iran
, signed on 17 June, provides a high-level basis for restoring maritime traffic, including commitments relating to the safe passage of commercial vessels during an initial period.
However, from a maritime perspective:
key implementation details remain unresolved
parties are understood to interpret aspects of the agreement differently
current operational conditions continue to be shaped by unilateral measures, including routing and insurance requirements
As a result, the agreement has enabled the start of evacuation operations but has not yet translated into a stable or unified transit framework.
Red Sea and Gulf of Aden: no material shift in underlying threat
The regional context outside the Strait remains broadly unchanged.
Assessments continue to indicate that:
Houthi forces retain the capability to conduct attacks against shipping in the Red Sea
targeting continues to be influenced by commercial linkages, particularly to Israel and the United States
the risk of misidentification and collateral damage persists even where incident activity is limited
This suggests that the current phase in the Strait of Hormuz does not materially alter the risk calculus for Red Sea and Gulf of Aden transits.
P&I perspective: transition to managed and conditional access
The current environment reflects a shift from disruption to managed but conditional access, with several key implications for shipowners and operators:
transit is subject to allocation, coordination and external approval, rather than shipowner discretion
routing decisions must account for overlapping and potentially conflicting authority structures
insurance and compliance requirements imposed by coastal states introduce additional exposure
interaction with naval forces and authorities remains a primary operational risk factor
In addition to physical risk, shipowners and operators should consider potential exposure related to:
compliance with local regulatory frameworks
sanctions and legal implications of engagement with designated entities
delay, deviation and associated liabilities
Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz is gradually resuming, but under conditions that differ fundamentally from pre-conflict operations.
The current system combines:
controlled evacuation
partial reopening
competing regulatory and enforcement mechanisms
This creates a fluid environment in which access is possible but conditional, and where operational predictability remains limited.
Update 9 June 2026
The operating environment across the Gulf region remains highly constrained, but the past week has brought two important shifts. First, transit through the Strait of Hormuz is increasingly being managed through a model of coordinated passage rather than physical escort. Secondly, the Red Sea threat picture has sharpened again following the Houthis’ announcement on 8 June that they have reinstated their ban on Israeli shipping.
Recent reporting from
JMIC
suggests that the immediate risk picture in the Strait has stabilised somewhat compared with the peak disruption phase, although the environment remains severe and highly sensitive to miscalculation, navigation interference and blockade-enforcement activity. In parallel, Risk Intelligence assesses that the broader strategic picture has not improved materially, with the United States and Iran continuing to exchange strikes while remaining entrenched in their respective positions.
Strait of Hormuz: severe, and no return to normality
The most material change in official reporting is that JMIC’s 7 June update reduced the regional threat level from CRITICAL to SEVERE, and also reduced the Strait of Hormuz from CRITICAL to SEVERE, explicitly citing the number of safe transits conducted via the southern route through Omani territorial waters. At the same time, JMIC continues to state that navigation interference, blockade-enforcement activity and mine-risk reporting remain relevant within and adjacent to the traffic separation scheme.
That reduction should not be read as a return to normal operating conditions. Traffic remains far below historical norms. JMIC’s 7 June advisory records only 6 transits on 4 June, 4 on 5 June and 3 on 6 June, against a historical average of around 138 vessels per day. It also continues to report persistent GNSS interference across the Strait approaches, the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Gulf.
JMIC, moreover, maintains that mine-risk reporting continues to apply within and adjacent to the TSS, referring again to the NAVAREA IX warning (180/26) issued on 26 May concerning a suspected floating mine. Vessels anchored close to the Strait may still be instructed to relocate, while short-notice actions by Iranian units remain possible.
Coordinated transit and the role of NCAGS
At present, the US Navy is not offering routine escorts for merchant shipping in the Strait of Hormuz. Instead, the model is one of coordination, situational awareness and deconfliction. Vessels seeking to transit are expected to engage proactively with the established reporting framework, including MSCHOA, UKMTO and, crucially, NAVCENT’s NCAGS cell, where practical interaction with US naval forces takes place. Within that framework, operators may share transit plans, receive routing guidance and benefit from real-time monitoring and potential military response in the event of an incident. The US Navy has worked to establish defined transit "pathways" based on demonstrated safe passages, with ships guided along these routes through communication rather than physical escort.
That means the current system is best understood as coordinated transit rather than convoying. As such, continuous communication remains central - vessels are expected to monitor VHF Channel 16, communicate their intentions clearly and comply immediately with instructions from naval units in the area.
For shipowners and operators, the practical implication is that early engagement with the coordination centres, close alignment with war risk insurers, and strict adherence to reporting and communications procedures remain essential. The
industry guidance
on the safe management of vessel transit through the Strait of Hormuz, produced by ICS, BIMCO, INTERCARGO, INTERTANKO, IMCA and OCIMF, remains directly relevant in this regard.
Recent Gulf developments: no sustained recovery and continued volatility
Although JMIC has reported no new verified incidents affecting commercial shipping in the Gulf of Oman in the most recent reporting periods, the wider operating environment remains unstable. On 1 June, JMIC recorded an attack on a cargo vessel 40 nautical miles south-east of Umm Qasr, Iraq, describing a large explosion following a hit from an unknown projectile on the starboard side. In its subsequent updates of 4 and 7 June, JMIC recorded no further incidents of that type, but retained SEVERE threat levels for the Arabian Gulf and Gulf of Oman.
Risk Intelligence added important strategic context - it states that the United States conducted strikes against Iranian facilities and claimed to have used a Hellfire missile against a vessel suspected of breaching the US naval blockade. It further states that Iran retaliated across the region, including against US military bases and port areas, and claimed to have attacked several vessels, including at least two operated by MSC in the region. Risk Intelligence’s core assessment is that these exchanges do not alter the underlying strategic picture, which remains one of periodic violence interspersed with suspended or largely performative negotiations.
Red Sea: renewed Houthi policy signal
The most important new development outside the Gulf itself is the renewed Houthi signal in the Red Sea. According to Ambrey’s 8 June threat circular, following escalation between Israel and Iran, the Houthis launched an attack against Israel on 8 June and, on the same day, military spokesperson Yahya Sare’e declared that the Houthis had reinstated their ban on Israeli shipping. Ambrey assesses Israel-owned, operated and managed shipping to be at high risk in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden. Ambrey also notes that companies calling Israel may face a realistic future targeting risk and that vessels calling Israel may be exposed to collateral damage.
This is an important development because it sharpens the distinction between the Gulf and Red Sea theatres. Ambrey explicitly states that threat levels in the Arabian Gulf, Strait of Hormuz and Gulf of Oman remain unchanged, while the immediate deterioration is concentrated on the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden for Israeli-linked interests.
That in turn broadly aligns with JMIC’s latest Red Sea reporting, which continues to assess the Bab el-Mandeb and Southern Red Sea as MODERATE, with no confirmed attacks on merchant shipping in the last 72 hours and no operational indicators of imminent targeting, even though Houthi rhetoric persists.
Multinational Military Mission: political support, but no immediate operational change
A further development that remains relevant is the emerging
Multinational Military Mission
for the Strait of Hormuz. The joint statement issued following the UK and French-led 12 May meeting confirms broad political support from multiple states for an independent, and strictly defensive mission intended to support civilian shipping, reassure commercial operators and conduct mine-clearance operations. The same statement also makes clear that operations will commence only in a permissive environment, and that the mission is intended to complement ongoing diplomatic and de-escalation efforts rather than replace them.
That limitation is important. For now, the Mission remains more significant politically than operationally. It signals sustained international concern over the security of commercial navigation through Hormuz, but it does not yet alter the practical reality faced by shipowners and masters.
Alongside that new initiative, the longer-standing European Maritime Awareness in the Strait of Hormuz (EMASoH) framework continues to matter as an instrument intended to protect freedom of navigation, ensure coordination among parties in the area and contribute to de-escalation through military and diplomatic presence. Its military component, Operation AGENOR, is focused on maritime situational awareness and surveillance rather than convoying.
P&I perspective: what matters for members now
From a P&I perspective, the current phase is defined less by mass casualty incidents and more by structured yet still fragile risk management under military oversight. The principal exposures remain:
miscalculation or misidentification in a congested and heavily monitored operating area
navigation risk arising from persistent GNSS interference and mine reporting
delay and deviation risk caused by restricted throughput and routing constraints
sanctions and compliance exposure where interactions with state-controlled transit systems or conflicting instructions arise
renewed war risk for Israel-linked shipping in the Red Sea following the Houthis' announcement of 8 June.
The more encouraging aspect of the latest reporting is that a model of coordinated transit through the Strait of Hormuz now appears more clearly established than in earlier phases of the crisis. The limiting factor is that this remains coordination-led, capacity-constrained and politically contingent, rather than a restored system of normal commercial passage.
Update 1 June 2026
Gulf Region update – 29 May 2026
The operating environment in the Gulf region remains highly constrained and complex. Since the previous update, the main developments include continued blockade enforcement activity, updated operational guidance from the Joint Maritime Information Center (JMIC), persistent mine risk in and around the Strait of Hormuz traffic separation scheme (TSS), and increased sanctions exposure linked to Iranian transit-control arrangements.
Current operating environment
According to
JMIC’s 31 May update
, the overall maritime threat level in the Middle East remains CRITICAL, with the Strait of Hormuz also assessed as CRITICAL, the Arabian Gulf and Gulf of Oman as SEVERE, and the Arabian Sea as MODERATE.
JMIC continues to report significantly reduced traffic levels through the Strait. Commercial vessels are routing south of the TSS via Omani territorial waters, while blockade enforcement, navigation interference and mine-risk concerns persist.
Risk Intelligence similarly assesses that the situation remains effectively stalled, with no clear pathway to a return to unrestricted transit. Instead, the operating model appears to be shifting towards controlled passage linked to Iranian transit arrangements.
Blockade enforcement and military activity
Recent JMIC advisories provide further clarity on the enforcement environment.
JMIC Advisory Note 006-26
states that the blockade of Iranian ports remains in force, and that vessels supporting ships in breach of the blockade - including through ship-to-ship operations - may also be considered non-compliant. The advisory highlights that enforcement measures may escalate to disabling or destructive fire where vessels do not respond appropriately to instructions. Mariners are advised to maintain a listening watch on VHF Channel 16 and clearly communicate intentions when requested by military forces.
JMIC Advisory Note 005-26
reports ongoing military operations north of the Musandam Peninsula, advising vessels to avoid the TSS, coordinate with
USNAVCENT NCAGS
, and comply immediately with directions from U.S. forces. The same advisory notes that vessels suspected of involvement in mine-laying may be treated as hostile and engaged in self-defence.
The practical implication is that exposure is not limited to direct attack risk, but also includes rapid escalation linked to delayed response, misidentification, or perceived non-compliance.
Mine risk and routing considerations
Mine risk remains a persistent concern. JMIC reports that a NAVAREA IX warning (180/26) was issued on 26 May following the sighting of a suspected floating mine in the Strait approaches, with vessels advised to proceed with extreme caution.
JMIC continues to assess that mine threats in and around the TSS remain credible, reinforcing earlier guidance to consider routing south of the TSS via Omani waters, where operationally feasible and aligned with official guidance.
The Strait should therefore still be treated as a managed and constrained transit area, rather than a corridor operating under normal conditions.
Incident picture and transit levels
Recent JMIC updates indicate no confirmed new attacks affecting commercial shipping within the Strait of Hormuz during the latest reporting periods. However, transit volumes remain materially reduced. JMIC reports daily transits in the range of 3–4 vessels, compared with  historical averages of approximately 138 vessels per day.
Earlier in the reporting period, JMIC and UKMTO reported an incident involving the tanker OLYMPIC LIFE east of Muscat, where an explosion was observed near the waterline. The crew was reported safe, and the cause remains under investigation.
UKMTO’s 26 May summary
states that it had received 52 incident reports across the Arabian Gulf, Strait of Hormuz and Gulf of Oman, including attack reports, suspicious activity, and hijack incidents.
Overall, the picture remains one of low traffic, no sustained recovery trend, and continued exposure to both direct and indirect operational risks.
Transit control, PGSA and sanctions exposure
A significant development is the growing convergence between transit control and sanctions risk.
Industry reporting and Risk Intelligence assessments indicate that Iran has formalised transit oversight through the Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA), positioning it as a central administrative interface for vessels seeking to transit the Strait of Hormuz. According to Maritime Executive reporting, the framework introduces a permit-based system, under which vessels are required to apply for transit authorisation, submit detailed operational information — including ownership, cargo, crew and insurance details, and align with Iranian-issued transit procedures before passage is granted.
The PGSA, therefore, represents a shift beyond ad hoc tolling towards a more structured traffic management and vetting regime, in which vessels may be screened and, based on submitted information, selectively approved or denied transit. This development effectively formalises Iranian oversight of vessel movements through the Strait, introducing both operational coordination requirements and additional compliance considerations for shipowners and their counterparties.
On 27 May, the
U.S. Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)
sanctioned the PGSA. OFAC states that engagement with the authority - including payments or receipt of transit-related services - may expose parties to sanctions risk, including for non-U.S. persons.
OFAC further highlights that risk may arise regardless of payment method, including indirect or non-cash arrangements.
This creates a complex operating environment in which compliance considerations may directly affect navigational decision-making, requiring careful assessment by shipowners, charterers and their counterparties.
Red Sea / Gulf of Aden
In the Southern Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb, JMIC reports no confirmed attacks on merchant vessels in recent reporting periods, with the overall threat level assessed as MODERATE.
However, both JMIC and Risk Intelligence continue to emphasise that the environment has not normalised, and that threat conditions can evolve rapidly. Risk Intelligence notes that any potential return to wider Red Sea transits remains driven by commercial and political considerations rather than a clear reduction in threat capability.
Considerations for Members and Assureds
In the current environment, Members and assureds should ensure that voyage planning reflects:
the potential for rapid shifts in enforcement posture and the need for immediate compliance with military instructions;
continued mine risk, particularly in and around the Strait approaches and TSS;
ongoing navigation interference, including GNSS disruption;
significantly elevated sanctions and compliance exposure, particularly in relation to transit authorisations and payment requests.
Voyage-specific risk assessments should incorporate both security and compliance factors, with clear onboard and shore-side alignment on routing, communications, and verification of instructions through recognised channels such as UKMTO and NAVCENT NCAGS.
Industry guidance issued by BIMCO, ICS, INTERCARGO, INTERTANKO, IMCA and OCIMF remains relevant, emphasising structured risk assessment, the Master’s overriding authority, and the potential for secondary navigational risks should traffic patterns shift.
Update 22 May 2026
Current operating environment
The
Joint Maritime Information Center
(JMIC) continues to assess the Arabian Gulf / Strait of Hormuz and the Gulf of Oman / Arabian Sea operating environment as CRITICAL, citing navigation interference, blockade enforcement activity, mine reporting and residual kinetic risk.
Traffic through the Strait remains significantly reduced, with continued high sensitivity to short-notice changes, and persistent congestion across anchorages.
Developments since 8 May: incidents and operating conditions
Since the 8 May update, JMIC has continued to record incidents affecting commercial shipping and to highlight the operational risks created by congestion, intermittent GNSS disruption, and mine reporting near the Traffic Separation Scheme (TSS).
Key reported incidents include:
8 May
An Indian-flagged dhow transiting from Dubai to Yemen caught fire and capsized; one crew member was reported dead, and 17 were rescued.
9 May

The Wider Gulf Region: Maritime security update
Since yesterday, several merchant vessels were struck in or near the approaches to the Strait of Hormuz. In the latest Joint Maritime Information Center (JMIC)
update
the following incidents have been reported:
SKYLIGHT (IMO 9330020) was reported hit about 5 nm north of Khasab (Oman) with four crew injuries and an evacuation.
MKD VYOM (IMO 9284386) suffered a projectile strike above the waterline with a fire brought under control.
An attack on SEA LA DONNA (IMO 9380532) is under investigation.
These incidents mark a shift from warnings to confirmed damage on multiple ships over the last 24–48 hours.
Dryad Global
has also assessed that sea mines remain a credible escalation option for Iran because even limited, quickly laid fields can produce disproportionate disruption in a narrow lane such as Hormuz - largely by eroding confidence and forcing slower, controlled movements, as seen during the Iran-Iraq “Tanker War” in 1984 to 1988.
However, this currently remains as an analytical risk, not a confirmed event as yet. There are still no reports of mines being laid, sighted, or detonated at the time of writing, a position that aligns with JMIC’s latest advisory notes.
As such, the immediate response at sea remains operator‑led - ships inside the Gulf have sheltered while others pause or divert away from the Strait of Hormuz. That behaviour reflects the confirmed strikes and ongoing electronic interference (GNSS/AIS/VHF) rather than any declared legal closure. Where sheltering has increased, routine risks rise - tight manoeuvring space, dragging anchors, and misunderstandings on VHF - especially near naval units.
Official and accredited guidance (unchanged but relevant to the new strikes).
US MARAD Maritime Alert 2026‑001A
remains in force: avoid the wider Hormuz/Gulf of Oman/North Arabian Sea area where possible and keep ≥ 30 nm clear of US naval units; maintain contact with NCAGS and monitor official outputs. This alert is consistent with the heightened risk now evidenced by the recent strikes.
UKMTO advisories
continue to emphasise caution, reporting, and awareness of electronic interference and misidentification risks; masters should keep a listening watch on VHF 16 and report abnormal activity promptly.
Following the confirmed vessel strikes, market reporting and security circulars indicate ongoing service suspensions/pauses and sheltering across liner trades serving the Middle East Gulf. This is now widely visible in schedules and anchorage density rather than policy statements.
What this means for shipowner and operators, today:
Plan for short‑notice traffic control in the approaches (possible speed reductions and controlled movements) if authorities initiate route sanitisation or clearance after any suspected mine-laying.
Where schedules allow, re‑routing remains the lower‑exposure choice versus extendeholding near chokepoints.
If sheltering, keep Closest Point of Approach (CPA) margins generous and radio work clear and professional, given the interference reports and close proximity of naval units.
Maintain full UKMTO/NCAGS reporting and preserve evidence (e.g., log extracts, ECDIS/VDR screenshots of GNSS anomalies, message receipts).
2 March 2026
The maritime risk picture has escalated markedly. The
Joint Maritime Information Center
(JMIC) now assesses the regional maritime threat as CRITICAL (an attack is
almost inevitable)
after confirmed missile/projectile strikes on multiple merchant vessels in the Gulf of Oman/Strait of Hormuz approaches within the past 24 hours. The
UKMTO
continues to describe the wider area as highly volatile, warning of sustained GNSS/AIS/VHF interference and noting that VHF claims of a “closure” of the Strait are unverified and not legally binding. In parallel, the
U.S. MARAD Maritime Alert 2026‑001A
advises vessels to avoid the region where possible and to maintain at least 30 nm separation from US naval units while remaining in contact with NCAGS and monitoring UKMTO/JMIC outputs.
Flag‑state postures have also tightened. The
Norwegian Maritime Authority
has raised MARSEC/ISPS Level 3 for Norwegian‑flagged vessels in the Persian Gulf, Strait of Hormuz and Gulf of Oman, signalling a probable or imminent security incident that requires further, specific protective measures under the ISPS Code.
Beyond Hormuz,
Lloyd’s List reports
the US Navy has established a broad maritime warning zone and cautions that Houthi retaliation in the Red Sea/Bab al‑Mandab cannot be excluded; the EU’s EUNAVFOR ASPIDES mission remains on heightened readiness. Taken together, there is a credible risk of dual‑theatre disruption (Hormuz and Red Sea/BaM) if these trends coincide.
Current situation: Strait of Hormuz, Gulf of Oman and adjacent waters
JMIC confirms three separate vessel attacks in the last 24 hours:
SKYLIGHT (IMO 9330020) was hit 5 nm north of Khasab with the crew evacuated and four injuries reported;
MKD VYOM (IMO 9284386) sustained a projectile strike above the waterline with the fire subsequently controlled;
SEA LA DONNA (IMO 9380532) reported an attack now under investigation.
JMIC adds that no sea mines have been detected, and it has identified no specific targeting rationale that would distinguish these ships from other commercial traffic.
Although many ships have received VHF hails asserting that the Strait is closed, there is no formal legal closure: JMIC notes no NAVAREA warning, no IMO Maritime Safety Information broadcast, and no charted exclusion zone.
The observed slowdown is a function of operator risk response rather than a promulgated suspension of transit passage. Even so, the throughput reduction is material: against a historic daily average of roughly 138 vessels, the most recent 24‑hour count is around 110.
Port‑side and coastal impacts underline the breadth of exposure. Jebel Ali recorded a debris‑related berth fire following an aerial interception, and anchorage density has increased at both approaches to the Strait; JMIC warns that clustering raises secondary risks - restricted manoeuvring space, anchor dragging, and collision exposure. Electronic warfare effects amplify those hazards: GNSS interference, AIS anomalies, and congested/contradictory VHF are widespread across the Gulf, the Gulf of Oman and Hormuz approaches.
Commercial operators are adapting quickly. Hapag‑Lloyd has suspended all transits of the Strait of Hormuz, while CMA CGM has ordered vessels inside or bound for the Gulf to proceed to shelter pending reassessment. On the financing side, JMIC reports a war‑risk market escalation, including
Notices of Cancellation
with buy‑back options subject to underwriting.
Red Sea/Bab al‑Mandab - Houthi activity and the “second theatre”
Authoritative industry reporting indicates the Houthis are signalling a resumption of missile/drone activity against maritime targets in the Red Sea/Gulf of Aden following US–Israeli strikes on Iran, ending a months‑long period of relative calm in late‑2025. MARAD’s standing advisory catalogues the group’s tactics, techniques and procedures - from one‑way UAVs/USVs/UUVs to cruise/ballistic missiles, explosive boats, small‑arms fire and seizures - and cautions that targeting has extended beyond vessels with clear Israeli/US affiliations to occasional misidentification and collateral strikes. In anticipation of renewed pressure along the Red Sea corridor, the EU has extended Operation ASPIDES to 28 February 2027, sustaining defensive coverage and situational awareness along the Bab al-Mandeb (BaM) corridor, even as resources remain finite.
The strategic concern is the overlap: a constrained Hormuz at the same time as renewed Red Sea/BaM activity yields a dual‑chokepoint problem with few workable maritime bypasses for Gulf‑bound cargo and heightened uncertainty for schedules, insurance and chartering.
What to do now
Refresh the Voyage Risk Assessment: before any approach to the affected waters, incorporating the latest JMIC, UKMTO, MARAD and flag‑state directives. Also factor explicitly for dual‑theatre exposure (Hormuz + Red Sea/BaM).
Operate per the security level in force: Norwegian‑flagged vessels: implement MARSEC/ISPS Level 3 measures in the Persian Gulf/Strait of Hormuz/Gulf of Oman; whereas other flags should escalate posture per CSO guidance and flag instructions.
Communicate and verify: Pre‑register/report with UKMTO; maintain a continuous VHF Ch.16 watch; respond professionally to directed hails; where practicable maintain ≥30 nm from U.S. naval units per MARAD. Treat unverified “closure” broadcasts as situational inputs, not legal restrictions - while still guarding against miscalculation near military units.
Navigate with redundancy: Expect
GNSS/AIS degradation
; cross‑check positions with radar ranges, visual bearings and echo sounder; preserve screenshots and VDR extracts when anomalies occur to support insurance and post‑incident analysis.
Manage density and timing: In congested approaches, plan generous CPAs, predictable courses/speeds and, where feasible, consider delaying, rerouting, or seeking escort/accompaniment recognising finite naval capacity (e.g., ASPIDES in Red Sea/BaM; situational awareness toward Hormuz).
