Russia – Wheat: Export Constriction and Baltic Port Realignment

In September 2026, Russian wheat exports decreased to 1.36 million tonnes, accompanied by a structural shift in logistics from the Azov-Black Sea basin toward Baltic Sea terminals.

By CropGPT Desk in · 1 Oct 2026, 01:00 · 3 min read

Published
1 Oct 2026
Last updated
2 Oct 2026
Reading time
3 min

Summary

The Impact is classified as Structural, as the restriction of primary deep-water port capacity to approximately 10% of its normal operational baseline forces a rerouting of over 50% of current export volumes.

The physical constriction of Black Sea logistics creates a binding bottleneck that prevents the clearance of record domestic wheat inventories.

Market Context

In September 2026, the Russian Federation exported 1.36 million tonnes of wheat, representing a 75% reduction compared to the 5.4 million tonnes exported during the same period last year. Active transshipment ports decreased from 45 to 22, and the number of active exporting entities contracted from 76 to 23.

Operations: Military strikes on Novorossiysk and Taman reduced active deep-water capacity to approximately 10% of normal levels. Export volumes via Novorossiysk contracted to 177,800 tonnes, down from 2.4 million tonnes a year prior. Conversely, Baltic Sea terminals handled 50% of September shipments, with Ust-Luga and Vysotsk processing 530,200 tonnes and 342,000 tonnes respectively. Trade: Wheat destination markets narrowed from 37 to 12 countries. Deliveries to primary markets contracted significantly, with shipments to Egypt dropping to 216,600 tonnes and Turkey dropping to 36,700 tonnes. Baltic and Caspian routes expanded to service Kenya, Saudi Arabia, Nigeria, and Iran. Policy: The government zeroed export duties on wheat through 31 December 2026 to stimulate outflows amidst rising domestic inventories.

Historically, baseline monthly shipments average between 4.8 and 4.9 million tonnes. The current reduction marks the lowest monthly export volume in at least nine years, establishing a severe divergence between domestic supply and executable export capacity.

Market Analysis

The removal of export duties is fundamentally misaligned with the physical execution crisis and will fail to accelerate total export volumes while Black Sea deep-water terminals remain restricted. Domestic wheat inventories stand at 29.94 million tonnes, up 10.1% year-on-year, alongside a 2026/27 harvest exceeding 90 million tonnes. The annual export target of 41.4 to 45 million tonnes requires a monthly clearance rate of approximately 3.5 million tonnes. Current infrastructure is clearing less than 40% of this requirement.

Evaluating logistical feasibility, the pivot to Baltic (Ust-Luga, Vysotsk) and Caspian (Astrakhan, Makhachkala) ports introduces acute cost-pressure proxies. Overland rail transportation from southern agricultural hubs to the Baltic ports extends supply chains significantly, eroding margins. Producer prices for fourth-class wheat contracted by 7.3% to 8,620 rubles (USD 103.3) per tonne, placing 28.5% of agricultural enterprises below the breakeven cost of 10,000 to 13,500 rubles (USD 120 to USD 161) per tonne. Maritime logistics face elevated premiums, with war-risk insurance at 2% of vessel value and freight rates to Southeast Asia increasing 29% to USD 54 per tonne. Internal rail subsidies are mathematically insufficient to bridge the gap between collapsed farm-gate prices and extended rail freight costs.

Tactical Supply Constraints

The incapacitation of Azov-Black Sea deep-water infrastructure forms a hard operational constraint. Alternate maritime nodes in the Baltic and Caspian seas possess structural capacity ceilings that cannot absorb the deficit left by Novorossiysk and Taman. Secondary constraints include a deficit in winter crop sowing for 2027, which currently sits at 6 million hectares against a target of 19 million, driven by waterlogged soils and deteriorating farm economics.

Outlook

The daily loading rate at the Novorossiysk and Taman terminals is the critical metric for the upcoming quarter. If deep-water Black Sea capacity remains constrained at current levels, the Baltic and Caspian diversion routes will reach maximum throughput capability, forcing tens of millions of tonnes of wheat to remain stranded in domestic storage. This will depress internal prices further, potentially triggering insolvency among highly leveraged producers and enforcing a sharp reduction in planted acreage for the 2027/28 season, regardless of state intervention targets.
