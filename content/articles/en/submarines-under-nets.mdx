<p className="lede">The Black Sea Fleet learned to hide its submarines under water inside their own harbour back in 2024. In the summer of 2026 a new trick was added on top: metal cages appeared over the sails, and a boom closed off the harbour entrance. It is the same logic that once produced improvised cages on tanks — except it has now reached the carriers of Caliber cruise missiles. And, more to the point, the tactic is no longer confined to the Black Sea.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">The measure</span>What exactly went up over the sails</h2>

<p>Not long ago the British Ministry of Defence put out two releases four days apart. The first, with imagery dated <strong>9 June 2026</strong>, recorded submarines lying alongside the quay wall, with counter-drone cages clearly visible over their sails. The second release, with imagery dated <strong>13 June</strong>, showed a different posture: the same boats submerged in the water in mid-basin<a className="ref" href="#ref-1">[1]</a>. The summary assessment, published on 4 August, reduces it to a figure: <strong>three of the four</strong> boats at Novorossiysk now have cages over their sails<a className="ref" href="#ref-2">[2]</a>.</p>

<figure className="fig">
  <img src="/articles/chovny-pid-sitkoyu/uk-mod-cages-260609.jpg" alt="Official British Ministry of Defence graphic: a satellite image of two Kilo-class submarines alongside a quay at Novorossiysk, with yellow callouts labelling the boats and the pale box structures of counter-drone cages over their sails" />
  <figcaption><strong>What a cage looks like · imagery of 9 June 2026.</strong> <em>Two boats alongside the quay wall, each with the pale box of a counter-drone cage over its sail. It is from frames like this that the count of protected boats is made.</em> UK MoD © Crown Copyright 2026 · imagery © Airbus DS 2026<a className="ref" href="#ref-1">[1]</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>The cage here is exactly what it looks like: a metal frame or mesh structure wrapping the <span className="term" data-def="The fairwater structure above a submarine's pressure hull, housing the retractable masts — periscope, communications antennas, snorkel induction.">sail</span> from above. No radar, no electronic warfare — a purely physical barrier, meant to make a drone break up or detonate on it before reaching the structure itself<a className="ref" href="#ref-2">[2]</a>.</p>

<p>The other solution is not new. In the 13 June imagery three boats are <strong>submerging into the water right inside the harbour on slackened mooring lines</strong>: most of the hull went under, leaving only the sails above the surface<a className="ref" href="#ref-1">[1]</a>. The fleet has been doing this since at least the spring of 2024: in a frame of Novorossiysk dated 17 March 2024 two boats are already lying in the water — but there are no cages over them yet.</p>

<p>But submersion is not a permanent state either. By <strong>17 June</strong>, four days after the British frame, the boats are back alongside the quay wall and afloat. This is neither a one-off nor a regime the fleet is stuck in: the boats settle into the water and come back up, and each such cycle is a trade-off between safety and readiness to put to sea.</p>

</figure>

    <li><strong>Semi-submersion</strong> — reduces the visible target area and takes the hull out of the line of fire.</li>
    <li><strong>A boom across the entrance</strong> — against what comes across the water.</li>
  </ul>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Kilo</span>Which boats we are actually looking at</h2>

<p>The British assessment speaks of "three of its four <em>Kilo</em> class submarines" — and that wording contains a nuance. <em>Kilo</em> is a NATO designation covering two different projects at once: the older 877 and the improved 636.3. Novorossiysk holds both, so "four Kilos" is not "four Project 636.3 Varshavyankas." The British do <strong>not name</strong> the boats precisely in the update: the text specifically cautions that names should not be assigned without additional imagery or movement data<a className="ref" href="#ref-2">[2]</a>.</p>

<p>The names, though, come from another source — Defense Express, which lists all four boats then present at Novorossiysk<a className="ref" href="#ref-4">[4]</a>. The list matches exactly what we set out in our <a href="/en/articles/four-years-at-sea-submarines">submarine chronicle</a> as of June 2026.</p>

<div className="tth">
  <h5>The four boats at Novorossiysk · summer 2026</h5>
  <div className="tth__row"><span className="k">B-262 Stary Oskol</span><span className="v">pr. 636.3 · active</span></div>
  <div className="tth__row"><span className="k">B-268 Veliky Novgorod</span><span className="v">pr. 636.3 · active</span></div>
  <div className="tth__row"><span className="k">B-271 Kolpino</span><span className="v">pr. 636.3 · probably damaged</span></div>
  <div className="tth__row"><span className="k">B-871 Alrosa</span><span className="v">pr. 877V · not a Caliber carrier</span></div>
</div>

<p>So the "four" Kilos are three Project 636.3 Varshavyankas plus Alrosa, a Project 877V boat. The rest of the Black Sea-assigned submarines fall outside this arithmetic: <em>Rostov-na-Donu</em> has been destroyed, while <em>Novorossiysk</em> and <em>Krasnodar</em> are at Kronstadt and are not returning to the Black Sea.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">The sail</span>Why that is what gets covered</h2>

<p>The choice of location is not arbitrary. The sail is not a submarine's fighting compartment and holds no launcher; the torpedo tubes sit in the bow, under water, where a drone has a rather harder time reaching them. What the sail does hold is everything without which the boat is blind and mute: the periscope, communications antennas, the snorkel induction mast and the other retractable equipment<a className="ref" href="#ref-2">[2]</a>.</p>

<p>This is equipment a crew cannot replace and that cannot be restored anywhere in the Black Sea. The fleet's repair base stayed in Sevastopol and under the constant threat of another strike, and the nearest yard able to service a Varshavyanka is at Kronstadt, reachable only through the straits. So a damaged sail means not a week of repairs but a boat out of service indefinitely.</p>

<p>How well this works is a separate question. Western analysis calls these structures <em>cope cages</em>, by analogy with the improvised grilles on Russian tanks. The name is mocking and judgemental. H. I. Sutton of Covert Shores, who specialises in submarines, rates the effect soberly: nets and cages give some protection against the smallest and slowest FPV quadcopters, but not against what has already been sinking ships in this sea<a className="ref" href="#ref-5">[5]</a>.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Kolpino</span>The boat that got no cage</h2>

<p>The most interesting thing here is not the three boats with cages but the fourth without one. Defense Express noticed the asymmetry and offered an explanation: if the unprotected boat is <strong>B-271 Kolpino</strong>, the reason may be simple. There is no point shielding it, because it is no longer operational<a className="ref" href="#ref-4">[4]</a>.</p>

<p>Our own chronicle reached the same conclusion. In our summary table Kolpino carries the note "probably damaged" — following operation <em>Sub Sea Baby</em> on 15 December 2025, when an underwater drone attacked the boat inside its base.</p>

<div className="callout callout--warn">
  <div className="callout__label">A hypothesis, not an established fact</div>
  Defense Express explicitly flags its reading as a version requiring confirmation by clearer imagery or official data. The British assessment does not name the boats at all, so the equation "uncaged boat = Kolpino" rests on an assumption. We present it as the most convincing explanation available, not as proven fact.
</div>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Layers</span>Booms, paint and false silhouettes</h2>

<p>Cages are only one of the methods of protection. Satellite imagery of the harbour published by MizarVision on 6 July 2026 shows two further measures. The first is a <strong>boom</strong>, strung in an arc across the entrance to the inner harbour: protection against the drone boats that have been working over ships in the port<a className="ref" href="#ref-6">[6]</a>.</p>

<figure className="fig fig--bleed">
  <img src="/articles/chovny-pid-sitkoyu/bon-vhid-havani.jpg" alt="High-resolution satellite image showing the entrance to the inner harbour at Novorossiysk closed off by an arc of floating boom running from the end of the mole to the quay wall" />
  <figcaption><strong>A boom across the harbour entrance.</strong> <em>The dotted arc running from the end of the mole to the opposite wall is a floating barrier against uncrewed surface vessels. It closes the only way into the inner harbour where the boats lie.</em> Satellite image: MizarVision · 07.2026<a className="ref" href="#ref-6">[6]</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>The other measure is <strong>repainting</strong>. Analysis of the same imagery indicates that some ships in the harbour have been repainted, and that painted silhouettes have appeared on the quays and the water, intended to confuse a drone operator or an automated classifier working on satellite imagery<a className="ref" href="#ref-6">[6]</a>. The trick is not new: deceptive camouflage on Black Sea Fleet ships was recorded as far back as 2023, and it did not prevent the strikes then either.</p>

<div className="methodology">
  <h4>Why MizarVision has to be handled with care</h4>
  <p>The MizarVision imagery comes from a platform where objects are marked up automatically and the accompanying text is labelled as model-generated. In one frame the system tagged <strong>five</strong> objects as "Admiral Grigorovich-class frigates." There are two Project 11356R frigates at Novorossiysk, and both are documented at a different pier.</p>
  <p>So this piece uses the MizarVision imagery as imagery, not as identification: the boom and the concealment measures are visible in the frame itself and corroborated by other analyses, while the names of the boats come from Defense Express.</p>
</div>

<h2><span className="h2-num">7,400 km</span>The same thing on Kamchatka</h2>

<p>If this were only about Novorossiysk it would be a story about one front-line base. But the netting has also appeared where Ukrainian drones do not reach. At any rate, not yet.</p>

<p>In May 2026 satellite imagery of the <strong>Rybachiy base on the Kamchatka Peninsula</strong> showed two ballistic missile submarines of Projects 955 and 955A Borei <strong>entirely covered in fine netting</strong>. That is 7,400 kilometres from Ukraine<a className="ref" href="#ref-5">[5]</a>. And the scale differs: at Novorossiysk the cage covers the sail alone, whereas at Rybachiy the netting is drawn over the entire hull. By August the method was being applied more widely: imagery from 4 August 2026 shows boats under netting on a large scale, erected by crane barges since May, with floating booms strung around the piers<a className="ref" href="#ref-7">[7]</a>.</p>

<figure className="fig fig--bleed">
  <img src="/articles/chovny-pid-sitkoyu/rybachiy-nets-260804.webp" alt="Satellite image of the Rybachiy base on Kamchatka: two large submarines alongside piers, covered along their full length by netting, one yellowish and one dark; a crane barge is working alongside" />
  <figcaption><strong>Rybachiy, Kamchatka · 4 August 2026.</strong> <em>Here the netting covers not the sail but the whole boat end to end — the mesh structure is clearly visible. A crane barge stands alongside the second boat: the work was still under way when the frame was taken.</em> Satellite image: Vantor<a className="ref" href="#ref-3">[3]</a>.</figcaption>
</figure>

<p>There is an explanation for the measure, and it is plain fear. After operation Spiderweb, when drones were launched from lorries beside the airfields themselves, a surprise attack from right under a base's nose stopped being hypothetical at any distance<a className="ref" href="#ref-5">[5]</a>. Whether covering an entire boat in netting is practical, especially in Kamchatka weather, is an open question that Russian sailors will have to work out in practice.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Bottom line</span>Protection in name</h2>

<p><strong>Whether these measures give even the slightest protection is the question.</strong> Against a few-hundred-dollar quadcopter they may well count for something. But against the weapons that have actually been putting ships out of action in this sea — cruise missiles, long-range jet drones, uncrewed surface vessels and underwater craft — a metal frame over the sail may be of little use. Kolpino was taken out by an underwater drone, and no netting overhead would have changed that.</p>

<p><strong>The price of the measure is readiness.</strong> A boat with a cage over its sail, lying in the harbour on slackened moorings, is not a combat unit at readiness but a suitcase without a handle. To move to a firing position and launch Calibers it has to surface, have the structure removed and then spend time preparing. Passive protection and reaction time are in direct conflict here.</p>

<p><strong>But it is worth noting that this is no longer a local improvisation.</strong> When the same netting appears over strategic missile submarines on Kamchatka, what is changing is the basing norm for the entire fleet: a ship in its own base is no longer treated as safe. Sutton put it more precisely than the official summaries — the protective moles of Novorossiysk are themselves becoming a cage.</p>
