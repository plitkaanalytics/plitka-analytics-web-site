<p className="lede">In earlier instalments of this series we showed how Russia's Black Sea Fleet turned the Caliber into its main tool for striking Ukraine — from frigates, small missile ships, and diesel-electric Varshavyankas. For decades the logic behind Russian shipbuilding has been the same: put as many long-range cruise-missile platforms as possible in every sea. Project 677 Lada is marketed as the next, technologically superior step in that same line.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Project 677</span>What Lada Is — and Why It Was Built</h2>

<p>Project 677 was developed by the Saint Petersburg design bureau TsKB MT Rubin in the 1990s under chief designer <strong>Yuri Kormilitsin</strong>; every boat in the series has been built at Admiralty Shipyards (part of the United Shipbuilding Corporation)<a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>. Lada was conceived as a universal replacement for the Project 877 Paltus-class Varshavyankas — with lower noise levels among the top requirements<a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>.</p>

<p>The project is considerably older than usually assumed: research and development on theme 677 began back in <strong>1987, in the USSR</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a> <span style={{ color: "var(--slate)" }}>(Tekhnika Molodezhi dates it to 1989<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>)</span>. The technical design was approved in 1993 — and immediately sent back for revision; the second version was only signed off in 1997, and <strong>180 separate development programmes</strong> were run in the course of creating the boat<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. Kormilitsin led the effort from the start; from 2008 the project's chief designer was <strong>Alexander Arsentyev</strong>, under whom the serial boats were completed<a className="ref" href="#ref15">[15]</a>. From the first research work to the flag-raising on the third boat in December 2025, <strong>38 years</strong> passed.</p>

<p>The original plan was to build Ladas widely: at the Leningrad Admiralty Association, Sevmash in Severodvinsk, Krasnoye Sormovo in Gorky, and the Lenin Komsomol yard in Komsomolsk-on-Amur. Sevmash even managed to accumulate a construction backlog for the lead order — after the collapse of the USSR it was handed to Admiralty Shipyards, and the whole programme came down to a single yard<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>.</p>

<p>By design it's a multi-purpose boat: destroying enemy surface ships and vessels, patrol duty, reconnaissance, protecting sea lines of communication, laying minefields<a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>. An important nuance that tends to get lost: Lada was designed first and foremost as an <strong>anti-submarine and patrol asset</strong>, not as a strike platform for cruise missiles — its role as a "Caliber carrier" became a deliberate talking point only later, riding the wave of enthusiasm for Calibers after 2015<a className="ref" href="#ref11">[11]</a>.</p>

<div className="qtbox">
  <div className="qtbox__lang">Design intent · chief designer</div>
  <p className="qtbox__quote">The submarine was conceived as a kind of underwater hunter, capable of destroying any target — surface combatants, transport vessels or submarines — by torpedo and missile strikes, by laying minefields, and also by means of combat divers.</p>
  <div className="qtbox__cite">Yuri Kormilitsin, general designer of Project 677<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a></div>
</div>

<div className="aside-note">
  <div className="aside-note__lbl">Terminology · why "non-nuclear" rather than "diesel-electric"</div>
  It was with Project 677 that the term <strong>"non-nuclear submarine"</strong> took hold in the Russian navy in place of the usual "diesel-electric." The reason is purely structural: the diesel no longer turns the shaft, it only generates electricity for the propulsion motor that actually drives the boat<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>.
</div>

<div className="callout">
  <p className="callout__title">Three Design Choices That Set Lada Apart</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>A compact hull architecture</strong> — a configuration Russia hadn't used on attack submarines since the 1940s<a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a>. Exactly which configuration is disputed: the Apalkov reference work calls the boat <strong>single-hull</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>, while N+1 describes it as <strong>one-and-a-half-hull</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref18">[18]</a>. Compared with Project 877, surface displacement is cut by roughly a factor of 1.3, noise is reduced, and submerged speed is higher<a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>The automated combat system "Litiy"</strong> and a high degree of automation, which cut the crew down to <strong>35</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>A permanent-magnet electric motor</strong> and a higher-capacity battery<a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a>.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div className="qtbox">
  <div className="qtbox__lang">Statement · Rubin</div>
  <p className="qtbox__quote">For the boat, "an entire line of new equipment — more than 130 items" was created, developed "on new principles."</p>
  <div className="qtbox__cite">Igor Vilnit, CEO of TsKB MT Rubin<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a></div>
</div>

<p>That figure is presented as an achievement, but in the same interview Vilnit explained its price: <strong>no money was allocated for bench-testing the equipment</strong>, so the first units went straight onto the boat and had to be debugged on board<a className="ref" href="#ref15">[15]</a>. More than a hundred and thirty systems never proven on a test bench, installed directly on the lead boat — that is the technical explanation for why Sankt-Peterburg took thirteen years to build and test.</p>

<div className="aside-note">
  <div className="aside-note__lbl">What the "range" line actually means</div>
  The rounded "~7,500 km" in the digests conflates two different regimes. Per Apalkov, Lada covers only <strong>650 nautical miles</strong> (about 1,200 km) fully submerged at economical speed — and <strong>6,000 miles</strong> while snorkelling, that is with the induction mast raised above the surface. In that regime the boat can be seen and heard. The ninefold gap between covert and semi-surfaced transit is the price of the missing air-independent propulsion discussed below.
</div>

<p>The defining structural feature sits in the definition of the type itself — and here two specialist sources disagree. The Apalkov reference work calls the boat <strong>single-hull</strong>, "with the minimum reserve buoyancy required to ensure surface unsinkability"<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. Tekhnika Molodezhi says the same: a single-hull scheme, one that "our designers returned to for the first time in quite a while"<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>. N+1 describes the same Lada as <span className="term" data-def="A scheme in which the pressure hull is only partly covered by a light outer hull that floods on diving and fills with air on surfacing.">one-and-a-half-hull</span>: the pressure hull is only partly covered by a light hull<a className="ref" href="#ref18">[18]</a>.</p>

<p>The practical contradiction is smaller than it looks: both descriptions say that the outer hull on Lada shrank sharply compared with the double-hull Varshavyankas. They differ on whether any of it remains. The consequence is the same either way — a direct trade: a smaller hull means less noise, a smaller signature, better manoeuvrability and faster diving, but also less reserve buoyancy if the hull is breached<a className="ref" href="#ref18">[18]</a>. Double-hull Varshavyankas are more survivable in that sense; the price of their survivability is volume and noise.</p>

<p>The powerplant was rewritten from scratch, and this is where most of the engineering novelty sits. Instead of DC diesel generators the boat has <strong>AC generators</strong>, and instead of two electric motors a single <span className="term" data-def="A motor that works efficiently across the whole range of revolutions, from creep to full speed. Previously a separate small motor was fitted for quiet economical running.">low-speed all-regime</span> propulsion motor, which made a separate creep motor unnecessary<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. Its full description is a <strong>multi-regime permanent-magnet main propulsion motor</strong>, quieter than conventional induction motors<a className="ref" href="#ref18">[18]</a>.</p>

<p>The diesel generator was created by the <strong>Kolomna plant</strong>. It has high specific power, works against high back-pressure, and is noticeably smaller and lighter than its predecessors. Its supercharging is adjustable and its built-in diagnostics catch faults at an early stage. Most importantly, a <strong>rapid battery-charging regime</strong> was developed around it, cutting the time the boat spends at periscope depth — its most vulnerable state<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>. The Russian MoD adds the designations: the SED-1 motor of 4,100 hp, D-49 diesels and 28DG generators of 2,100 kW each<a className="ref" href="#ref17">[17]</a>. The D-49 itself is a <strong>medium-speed eight-cylinder V-type diesel with a 260 mm bore and 260 mm stroke, rated at a maximum 1,000 rpm</strong>. Power is supplied by two battery groups of 120 cells each; the propulsor is a single low-noise <strong>seven-bladed propeller with scimitar blades</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a> (for comparison, the Project 877V Alrosa has a pump-jet). Two retractable thrusters with a 102 kW drive are fitted in case the main line fails.</p>

<div className="callout">
  <p className="callout__title">Firsts for Russian submarine construction</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Forward diving planes on the sail</strong> — a layout used for the first time on a Russian diesel-electric boat<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>Telescopic retractable masts</strong> that <strong>do not penetrate the pressure hull</strong> — with the exception of the commander's periscope. That removes a series of hull openings, each of them a potential leak and a noise source<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>The Parus periscope</strong> developed by TsNII Elektropribor — with optical and low-light television channels<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>A new-generation anti-sonar coating</strong> created at the Krylov institute; together with sound-absorbing elements and the mounting of all machinery on shock absorbers it is claimed to cut the acoustic signature several times over compared with Projects 877 and 636<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>Living conditions</strong> — the accommodation compartment as a "cabin-type hotel" with a personal bunk for every sailor and a wardroom seating half the crew at once<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div className="callout callout--warn">
  <span className="callout__label">The equipment that has names</span>
  <p>Open reference works and Russian MoD releases give specific designations rather than a generic "electronic suite": the <strong>Lira</strong> sonar suite with its <strong>L-01</strong> bow array. The combat information and control system is <strong>Litiy</strong>, the radar is <strong>KRM-66 Kodak</strong>, and there are two <strong>Parus-98</strong> periscopes — the commander's KP and the universal UP<a className="ref" href="#ref17">[17]</a>. Sources disagree about <strong>Murena</strong>: the reference work calls it a torpedo fire-control system<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>, while Tekhnika Molodezhi describes it as the automatic rapid silent reloading gear for the torpedo tubes, which also have a pneumatic launch system<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>. The navigation suite differs between the two: <strong>Appassionata</strong> with an inertial system and GPS/GLONASS in the Kronshtadt release, against <strong>Andoga</strong> in the reference work for the baseline project; most likely this is the difference between the baseline 677 and the modernised 677M. Two of the six torpedo tubes are adapted for <strong>wire-guided</strong> torpedoes — controlled by an operator over a trailing wire after launch. And one thing rarely mentioned in accounts of Lada: the boat carries its own air defence — <strong>Igla-1M</strong> MANPADS with six missiles in a single stowage container.</p>
</div>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Sensors</span>Betting on Sonar</h2>

<p>Lada's key claimed advantage isn't missiles — it's "ears." The suite is designated <strong>Lira</strong>, and its large-aperture bow passive array carries the index <strong>L-01</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref17">[17]</a>. The boat also carries a <strong>towed array</strong> and, by available accounts, a large <span className="term" data-def="A flexible array built into the hull's outer contour rather than a separate dome — it gives a larger receiving surface without adding drag.">conformal array</span> in the bow<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. Naval analyst H. I. Sutton has assessed that this bow conformal array may be the largest of its type on any non-nuclear submarine<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</p>

<p className="pullquote pullquote--soft">If the specifications hold up, it's the sensors — not the strike potential — that could give Lada an edge: detecting other submarines before it is detected itself.<cite>Consistent with the project's original anti-submarine mission<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a></cite></p>

<p>Low noise, according to Rubin's CEO, rests on three things: purpose-built quiet machinery, an external anti-sonar coating and carefully designed hull lines<a className="ref" href="#ref15">[15]</a>. One more measure belongs on that list, one Rubin did not mention: all equipment capable of vibrating in operation is mounted on <strong>shock absorbers</strong> — decoupled from the hull so the vibration does not reach the water<a className="ref" href="#ref18">[18]</a>. The aft fairing for the towed array channel is visible on the longitudinal section: the flexible array is literally streamed behind the boat, away from its own noise<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>.</p>

<p>In the same interview Vilnit explained why a powerful sonar is not just an antenna. <strong>The more sensitive the set, the more extraneous noise it picks up along with the useful signal</strong>, so everything depends on a "high-speed" data processing system. And detecting a target is only half the job: it then has to be classified, its speed, depth and course determined — while your own boat, the target and the medium itself are all moving<a className="ref" href="#ref15">[15]</a>.</p>

<div className="qtbox">
  <div className="qtbox__lang">Statement · Russian Navy</div>
  <p className="qtbox__quote">If the Project 877 submarine was called the "Black Hole," then let us call this one the "Invisible." Nobody will really see or hear it.</p>
  <div className="qtbox__cite">Admiral Viktor Chirkov, Commander-in-Chief of the Russian Navy 2012–2016 — as recounted by Vilnit<a className="ref" href="#ref15">[15]</a></div>
</div>

<p>According to Rubin, during trial service Sankt-Peterburg detected low-noise nuclear and non-nuclear submarines of other projects, as well as surface targets, and shadowed them covertly "for extended periods"<a className="ref" href="#ref15">[15]</a>. There is no independent confirmation of this, and the lead boat itself was sent for scrapping four years after those words.</p>

<p>The real performance of Russia's newest sonar suites is not reliably known. But that, not the missile salvo, is where the design logic actually sits — a quiet diesel-electric "hunter" for confined coastal waters.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Weapons</span>Caliber from a Torpedo Tube: 10 vs. 4</h2>

<p>Strike potential is what makes Lada interesting as a threat. The boat has six 533 mm torpedo tubes; the load-out is up to 16–18 torpedoes and missiles or up to 44 mines, and the claimed Caliber capacity is <strong>up to ten missiles</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref7">[7]</a>. The same tubes fire the anti-ship Klub and the anti-submarine <span className="term" data-def="RPK-6 Vodopad (NATO reporting name SS-N-16) — an anti-submarine missile launched from a 533 mm torpedo tube that delivers a torpedo or depth charge to the target area.">RPK-6 Vodopad</span><a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</p>

<p>For comparison: the Project 636.3 Varshavyankas that have actually fired at Ukraine and Syria carry only <strong>four</strong> Calibers each<a className="ref" href="#ref10">[10]</a>. On paper, then, one Lada equals roughly two or three Varshavyankas by missile salvo — and that is the whole substance of the "new generation of carriers" narrative.</p>

  <img src="/articles/lada-proekt-677/nis-torpedni-aparaty.jpg" alt="Close-up of the bow of a Project 677 Lada submarine: torpedo tube shutters flush with the plating and the anti-sonar coating" />
  <figcaption><strong>The bow: six shutters and the coating.</strong> <em>The torpedo tube shutters sit flush with the plating so as not to create turbulence or additional reflection. The anti-sonar coating Rubin spoke about is visible too: it is laid in separate tiles.</em></figcaption>
</figure>

<div className="callout callout--warn">
  <span className="callout__label">The structural catch · there are no vertical launchers</span>
  <p>The serial Lada <strong>has no vertical launch system</strong> — every missile goes out through a torpedo tube<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. That limits the rate of fire and forces the load-out to be divided between missiles, torpedoes and mines. The paradox: it was precisely a VLS that Russia offered <strong>to foreign customers</strong> in the export version, while never giving it to its own boats.</p>
</div>

<figure className="fig">
  <img src="/articles/lada-proekt-677/amur-950-vpu.jpg" alt="Promotional cutaway of the export Amur-950 submarine showing torpedoes and ten vertical missile launchers" />
  <figcaption><strong>Where the "ten" came from.</strong> <em>A promotional cutaway with the manufacturer's own caption: "Submarine Amur-950, armed with torpedoes and 10 vertical missile launchers." Ten launchers is a parameter of the <strong>export</strong> boat that was never built — not of the Ladas serving with the Russian Navy.</em></figcaption>
</figure>

<div className="callout callout--warn">
  <span className="callout__label">How many missiles, and which: four accounts that don't add up</span>
  <p>Descriptions of Project 677's missile armament vary from source to source. The <strong>Apalkov reference work</strong> gives USET-80K torpedoes and Club-S anti-ship missiles for the baseline project — no Caliber<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. The <strong>Russian MoD</strong>, on the day Kronshtadt was accepted in January 2024, named the supersonic anti-ship <strong>P-800 3M55 Oniks</strong> and <strong>P-900 3M51 Alfa</strong> plus torpedoes and mines — again without Caliber<a className="ref" href="#ref17">[17]</a>. <strong>English-language digests</strong> say "up to ten Calibers"<a className="ref" href="#ref10">[10]</a>. And finally the <strong>builder</strong>: in February 2021 Alexander Buzakov, CEO of Admiralty Shipyards, stated that the serial boats would be armed with <strong>launchers for Caliber cruise missiles</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref19">[19]</a>.</p>
  <p>Most of these discrepancies dissolve on two facts. First: Club-S (Russian designation <strong>Biryuza</strong>, NATO reporting name SS-N-27 Sizzler), developed by the Novator design bureau, is not an alternative to Caliber but <strong>the same missile family under its export name</strong>. The complex comprises the anti-ship 3M-54E and 3M-54E1, the anti-submarine 91RE1 and 91RE2, and the land-attack <strong>3M-14E</strong> — the export variant of the very 3M-14 the Varshavyankas fire at Ukraine<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>. Second: the serial boats are built to a modernised design, and it is Kronshtadt and Velikie Luki that received Caliber, a more powerful and quieter propulsion motor and updated onboard systems, none of which the lead boat Sankt-Peterburg had<a className="ref" href="#ref19">[19]</a>.</p>
  <p>The number <strong>ten</strong> needs a separate explanation. It appears in English-language digests and on Wikipedia, but no specialist reference work confirms it. The promotional cutaway of the export Amur-950, on the other hand, carries the manufacturer's caption: a boat "armed with torpedoes and 10 vertical missile launchers" (see above). Ten is a parameter of the <strong>export design with vertical launchers</strong> that was never built, not of the boats in Russian naval service.</p>
  <p>The MoD's own list does not add up either. Oniks is a large missile roughly 670 mm in diameter, normally launched from vertical or inclined launchers; it will not physically fit a 533 mm torpedo tube, and the serial Lada has no vertical launchers. The 3M51 Alfa, by contrast, was developed for torpedo-tube launch in the first place. So <strong>the claimed missile fit for Lada does not match its own claimed launchers</strong>, and the likeliest explanation is the same one — the export Amur-950's characteristics transplanted onto a serial boat.</p>
</div>

<h2><span className="h2-num">AIP</span>The Air-Independent Propulsion That Isn't There</h2>

<p>The technology the whole project was started for was <span className="term" data-def="Air-independent propulsion (AIP) lets a diesel submarine run submerged for long periods without surfacing to start its diesels, sharply reducing the risk of detection.">air-independent propulsion (AIP)</span> — it was meant to sharply increase submerged endurance and make the boat harder to detect. The irony is that the USSR and Russia had groundwork in this field going back to the 1950s (the experimental Project 617)<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</p>

<p>On Sankt-Peterburg, which served as the prototype, the installation never worked, and AIP for the series was effectively <strong>abandoned</strong> — in 2019 Rubin stated outright that the boats of the series would not be fitted with it<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a>. A parallel line of work was under way, however, and it had entirely specific executors. In early 2018 it was reported that the <strong>Central Research Institute of Marine Electrical Engineering and Technology, together with the Krylov State Research Centre</strong>, would complete development of an anaerobic power plant for submarines <strong>by 2020</strong> — and that the first such installation would be fitted to the lead Lada, Sankt-Peterburg. The claimed effect: submerged endurance of <strong>up to 45 days</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref18">[18]</a>. In that same year, 2019, development resumed on an electrochemical generator — extracting hydrogen from diesel fuel and oxygen — with completion "in the mid-2020s." Lithium-ion batteries were also discussed as an alternative that would in theory give a diesel boat very long submerged endurance<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a>.</p>

<p>As of 2026, <strong>no AIP has been installed on any of the boats built</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref5">[5]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref11">[11]</a>. Without it Lada remains noisier and easier to detect — the new generation's headline advantage is still on the drawing board. Western analysts consistently describe the programme as a "disappointment"<a className="ref" href="#ref5">[5]</a>.</p>

<p>In a lengthy interview given ahead of Kronshtadt's acceptance in January 2024, Rubin's CEO <strong>does not mention air-independent propulsion once</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref15">[15]</a>. He speaks of "greater submerged endurance" as a designer's goal — but does not explain how it is achieved on a boat that covers 650 miles submerged at three knots. The technology the project was begun for was passed over in silence at the moment of its principal triumph.</p>

<div className="callout callout--warn">
  <span className="callout__label">The series was cancelled twice — and revived twice</span>
  <p>Stated plans for the number of boats changed as sharply as the decision to build the series at all. At the lead boat's launch in 2004, navy representatives promised a run of <strong>up to forty</strong> submarines, and the Baltic Fleet was to have two brigades of six non-nuclear boats each by 2020<a className="ref" href="#ref20">[20]</a>. In March 2009 Vice Admiral Oleg Burtsev, deputy chief of the Navy Main Staff, spoke of a series of <strong>eight</strong>; in November 2018 Navy Commander-in-Chief Admiral Vladimir Korolyov spoke of <strong>twelve</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. Between those two statements came the 2009 pause, when construction of the second and third boats was halted by Defence Ministry decision, and — most importantly — <strong>on 19 January 2016 RIA Novosti reported that Project 677 boats would not be built at all</strong>, with funding redirected to the prospective Kalina project<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. The decision was then reversed and the two boats already laid down were completed. In January 2024, accepting Kronshtadt, the MoD was speaking of <strong>six</strong> boats in the series<a className="ref" href="#ref17">[17]</a>. Deadlines slipped independently of numbers: in February 2021 Admiralty Shipyards CEO Alexander Buzakov promised to hand both serial boats to the fleet <strong>in 2022</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref19">[19]</a> — Kronshtadt was delivered in 2024 and Velikie Luki in 2025.</p>
  <p>The latest figure came from Putin himself. On <strong>24 July 2025</strong>, at a meeting on the development of the submarine forces held in Severodvinsk, Arkhangelsk Oblast, he said: "Construction of Project 677 non-nuclear submarines is under way. The lead submarine Kronshtadt was accepted into the Navy back in 2024. A total of no fewer than nine such submarines are planned"<a className="ref" href="#ref22">[22]</a>. Two separate claims sit in those two sentences, and both are worth noting.</p>
  <p>The first is <strong>yet another change to the target figure</strong>: no fewer than nine, against the six the Defence Ministry had been citing eighteen months earlier. The second is subtler: <strong>the lead boat of the project is now said to be Kronshtadt</strong>. The lead boat was and remains B-585 Sankt-Peterburg — the one that took thirteen years to build and that had been struck off for scrap eighteen months before this speech, its sonar and propulsion beyond repair. Reassigning the title of "lead boat" to the first serial hull removes the project's most awkward episode from its official history.</p>
  <p>The actual position at the end of August 2026: six boats laid down over 28 years of the programme, of which <strong>two are combat-capable</strong> — Kronshtadt and Velikie Luki, each of which took between ten and nineteen years. Three more sit on the slipways. To deliver on "no fewer than nine," Russia would have to contract and lay down at least three or four more submarines — and that is without counting the scrapped Sankt-Peterburg.</p>
</div>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Export</span>Amur-1650 and Amur-950: The VLS Denied to Russia's Own Navy</h2>

<p>Russia tried to recoup its costs by selling the design abroad. The export versions were designated <strong>Amur-1650</strong> and <strong>Amur-950</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>. Notably, the Amur-950 was offered with a <strong>vertical launch system</strong> for cruise missiles (BrahMos among them) — that is, foreign buyers were offered a better-armed configuration than the one Russia's own fleet ended up with<a className="ref" href="#ref11">[11]</a>.</p>

<p>The main prospective customer was India, which was offered joint production. India chose the French design, and the Amur export programme effectively died — no other buyers were found<a className="ref" href="#ref11">[11]</a>. That hits both the economics of the programme and its reputation.</p>

<div className="callout callout--warn">
  <span className="callout__label">The export boat nobody has seen</span>
  <p>The Amur exists as more than a paper design. The export boat was laid down at Admiralty Shipyards <strong>on the very same day as Sankt-Peterburg, 26 December 1997</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. According to ITAR-TASS, by 2005 it was 47 per cent complete, and by 2009 construction had stopped altogether<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. The export Lada has therefore stood unfinished for almost thirty years — and for thirty years buyers have been offered something that does not physically exist in completed form. Besides the Amur-1650, a whole family of Amur variants differing in displacement and propulsion was developed for export; not one was ever built<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>.</p>
</div>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Outlook</span>The Program's Future — Staying the Conventional Course</h2>

<p>For all Lada's problems, the Kremlin's general line is to stay with conventional submarines (unlike, say, the United States, which builds only nuclear boats)<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. Russia's Defence Ministry claims the fleet has received 24 submarines of various projects over ten years — eight times more than in the preceding decade<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. Yet it is precisely Lada's delays that force Russia to keep building the less capable Project 636.3 Varshavyankas that Lada was meant to replace<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</p>

<div className="stat-2col">
  <div className="stat-2col__cell">
    <div className="stat-2col__lbl">The Kremlin's claim</div>
    <ul>
      <li>A series of <strong>no fewer than nine</strong> boats — stated by Putin himself in July 2025<a className="ref" href="#ref22">[22]</a>.</li>
      <li>One Lada is claimed to carry <strong>up to ten Calibers</strong> — two to three times a Varshavyanka<a className="ref" href="#ref7">[7]</a>.</li>
      <li>Caliber's range — roughly 1,500–2,500 km by CSIS estimates<a className="ref" href="#ref9">[9]</a>: a boat in the Baltic theoretically holds much of Europe at risk.</li>
      <li>Serial production is "under way": keel-layings continue.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div className="stat-2col__cell">
    <div className="stat-2col__lbl">What actually exists</div>
    <ul>
      <li>Afloat and combat-capable — <strong>two</strong>: Kronshtadt and Velikie Luki. Each took between ten and nineteen years.</li>
      <li>On the slipways — <strong>three</strong> unfinished: Vologda, Yaroslavl, Maloyaroslavets.</li>
      <li>Reaching nine would need <strong>three or four</strong> more boats that are not yet even under contract.</li>
      <li>Not one boat has received either <strong>air-independent propulsion</strong> or <strong>vertical launchers</strong> for Caliber.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<div className="callout">
  <p className="callout__title">Lada and the Black Sea</p>
  <p>The question of whether Lada threatens Ukraine was answered by the designer itself — back in December 2010. Andrei Dyachkov, then CEO of TsKB MT Rubin, stated in an interview that <strong>the Black Sea is too small for Project 677 boats</strong>, and that Ladas would therefore not join the Black Sea Fleet<a className="ref" href="#ref16">[16]</a>. This is the position of the design bureau that created the boat. Lada was designed for the deep water of the north and the specifics of the Baltic; the enclosed shallow sea where the Varshavyankas operate was considered an unsuitable theatre for it long before any of the current events.</p>
</div>

<p>On top of that, the Baltic and the North Atlantic are areas of dense NATO surveillance, where a small diesel submarine finds it harder to manoeuvre unnoticed than in the enclosed Black Sea.</p>

<p className="pullquote">Lada is indeed becoming a new diesel-electric Caliber carrier — but far more slowly, in far smaller numbers and with worse characteristics than Moscow declares.<cite>Less a "new generation of threats" than a protracted build with high paper potential and minimal delivery</cite></p>

<p>The central question is not the boat's specification-sheet capability but the capacity of Russian industry to build such boats in series, and to a decent standard, under war and sanctions<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. If Russia ever gets AIP (or the lithium-ion variant) working and reaches series production, the picture will need revisiting; for now Lada's real contribution to the fleet's strike potential is minimal.</p>
