<p className="lede">In earlier instalments of this series we showed how Russia's Black Sea Fleet turned the Caliber into its main tool for striking Ukraine — from frigates, small missile ships, and diesel-electric Varshavyankas. For decades the logic behind Russian shipbuilding has been the same: put as many long-range cruise-missile platforms as possible in every sea. Project 677 Lada is marketed as the next, technologically superior step in that same line.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Project 677</span>What Lada Is — and Why It Was Built</h2>

<p>Project 677 was developed by the Saint Petersburg design bureau TsKB MT Rubin in the 1990s under chief designer <strong>Yuri Kormilitsin</strong>; every boat in the series has been built at Admiralty Shipyards (part of the United Shipbuilding Corporation)<a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>. Lada was conceived as a universal replacement for the Project 877 Paltus-class Varshavyankas — with lower noise levels among the top requirements<a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>.</p>

<p>By design it's a multi-purpose boat: destroying enemy surface ships and vessels, patrol duty, reconnaissance, protecting sea lines of communication, laying minefields<a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>. An important nuance that tends to get lost: Lada was designed first and foremost as an <strong>anti-submarine and patrol asset</strong>, not as a strike platform for cruise missiles — its role as a "Caliber carrier" became a deliberate talking point only later, riding the wave of enthusiasm for Calibers after 2015<a className="ref" href="#ref11">[11]</a>.</p>

<div className="callout">
  <p className="callout__title">Three Design Choices That Set Lada Apart</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Single-hull architecture</strong> — a configuration Russia hadn't used on attack submarines since the 1940s<a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a>. Compared with Project 877, surface displacement is cut by roughly a factor of 1.3, noise is reduced, and submerged speed is higher<a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>The automated combat system "Litiy"</strong> and a high degree of automation, which cut the crew down to <strong>35</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</li>
    <li><strong>A permanent-magnet electric motor</strong> and a higher-capacity battery<a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a>.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div className="qtbox">
  <div className="qtbox__lang">Statement · Rubin</div>

  <div className="qtbox__cite">Igor Vilnit, CEO of TsKB MT Rubin<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a></div>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Sensors</span>Betting on Sonar</h2>

<p>Lada's key claimed advantage isn't missiles — it's "ears." The boat carries a <strong>towed sonar array</strong>, and, by available accounts, a large <span className="term" data-def="A flexible array built into the hull's outer contour rather than a separate dome — it gives a larger receiving surface without adding drag.">conformal array</span> in the bow<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. Naval analyst H. I. Sutton has assessed that this bow conformal array may be the largest of its type on any non-nuclear submarine<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</p>

<p className="pullquote pullquote--soft">If the specifications hold up, it's the sensors — not the strike potential — that could give Lada an edge: detecting other submarines before it is detected itself.<cite>Consistent with the project's original anti-submarine mission<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a></cite></p>

<p>The real performance of Russia's newest sonar suites is not reliably known. But that, not the missile salvo, is where the design logic actually sits — a quiet diesel-electric "hunter" for confined coastal waters.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Weapons</span>Caliber from a Torpedo Tube: 10 vs. 4</h2>

<p>Strike potential is what makes Lada interesting as a threat. The boat has six 533 mm torpedo tubes; the load-out is up to 16–18 torpedoes and missiles or up to 44 mines, and the claimed Caliber capacity is <strong>up to ten missiles</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref7">[7]</a>. The same tubes fire the anti-ship Klub and the anti-submarine <span className="term" data-def="RPK-6 Vodopad (NATO reporting name SS-N-16) — an anti-submarine missile launched from a 533 mm torpedo tube that delivers a torpedo or depth charge to the target area.">RPK-6 Vodopad</span><a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</p>

<p>For comparison: the Project 636.3 Varshavyankas that have actually fired on Ukraine and Syria each carry only <strong>four</strong> Calibers<a className="ref" href="#ref10">[10]</a>. On paper, one Lada is worth roughly two to three Varshavyankas in missile salvo terms — and that comparison is the whole point of the "new-generation carrier" narrative.</p>

<div className="callout callout--warn">
  <span className="callout__label">The Structural Catch · No Vertical Launch</span>
  <p>The production Lada <strong>has no vertical launch system (VLS)</strong> — every missile goes out through a torpedo tube<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. That caps salvo rate and forces a trade-off between missiles, torpedoes, and mines in the load-out. The irony: Russia offered VLS to <strong>foreign buyers</strong> in the export variant (see below), but never gave it to its own fleet.</p>
</div>

<h2><span className="h2-num">AIP</span>The Air-Independent Propulsion That Isn't There</h2>

<p>The technology the whole project was built around was <span className="term" data-def="Air-independent propulsion (AIP) lets a diesel-electric submarine stay submerged for long periods without surfacing to run its diesels, sharply reducing the risk of detection.">air-independent propulsion (AIP)</span> — meant to sharply extend submerged endurance and make the boat much harder to find. The irony is that the USSR/Russia had a head start here going back to the 1950s (the experimental Project 617)<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</p>

<p>But on Sankt-Peterburg, which served as the prototype, the system never worked, and AIP was effectively <strong>dropped</strong> for the series — in 2019 Rubin stated outright that the boats would not be fitted with it<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a>. Instead, development resumed that same year on an electrochemical generator (extracting hydrogen from diesel fuel and oxygen), targeted for completion "in the mid-2020s"; a lithium-ion battery alternative was also discussed, one that could theoretically give a diesel-electric boat very long submerged endurance<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref14">[14]</a>.</p>

<p>As things stand, <strong>none of the boats built so far has AIP installed</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref5">[5]</a><a className="ref" href="#ref11">[11]</a>. Without it, Lada stays noisier and easier to detect — meaning the new generation's headline advantage is still on the drawing board. Western analysts consistently describe the program as a "disappointment"<a className="ref" href="#ref5">[5]</a>.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Export</span>Amur-1650 and Amur-950: The VLS Denied to Russia's Own Navy</h2>

<p>Russia tried to recoup costs by selling the project abroad. The export versions were named <strong>Amur-1650</strong> and <strong>Amur-950</strong><a className="ref" href="#ref1">[1]</a>. Notably, the Amur-950 was offered with a <strong>vertical launch system</strong> for cruise missiles (including BrahMos) — meaning foreign buyers were offered a better-armed configuration than the one Russia's own fleet ultimately received<a className="ref" href="#ref11">[11]</a>.</p>

<p>The main prospective customer was India, which was offered joint production. India chose a French design instead, and the Amur export program effectively died — no other buyers materialized<a className="ref" href="#ref11">[11]</a>. That hurt both the program's economics and its reputation.</p>

<h2><span className="h2-num">Outlook</span>The Program's Future — Staying the Conventional Course</h2>

<p>Despite all of Lada's problems, the Kremlin's overall line is to keep building conventional submarines (unlike, say, the US, which builds only nuclear boats)<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. Russia's Ministry of Defence claims the fleet received 24 submarines of various types over the past decade — eight times more than the decade before<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. But it's precisely because of Lada's delays that Russia is still stuck building the less capable Project 636.3 Varshavyankas that Lada was meant to replace<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>.</p>

<div className="stat-2col">
  <div className="stat-2col__cell">
    <div className="stat-2col__lbl">On paper — a "new generation"</div>
    <ul>
      <li>One Lada is claimed to carry <strong>up to 10 Calibers</strong> — two to three times a Varshavyanka's load.</li>
      <li>Caliber's range (up to ~2,500 km) means a boat sailing from the Baltic could theoretically hold much of Europe at risk.</li>
      <li>A large conformal sonar array — as claimed, a serious anti-submarine asset in confined waters.</li>
      <li>The program hasn't been shelved: new boats are being laid down in 2026.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div className="stat-2col__cell">
    <div className="stat-2col__lbl">In practice — a stalled build</div>
    <ul>
      <li>28 years in, only <strong>two or three</strong> combat-capable boats; the lead boat was scrapped.</li>
      <li>The key technology (<strong>AIP</strong>) was never delivered; there's no VLS.</li>
      <li>Not a single Lada <strong>has fired a Caliber in combat</strong> throughout the entire war — unlike the Varshavyankas.</li>
      <li>The export program failed, and sanctions are squeezing component supply.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<p>On top of that, the Baltic and the North Atlantic are zones of dense NATO surveillance, where a small diesel-electric submarine has a much harder time maneuvering undetected than in the closed Black Sea.</p>

<p className="pullquote">Lada is genuinely becoming a new diesel-electric Caliber carrier — just far more slowly, in far smaller numbers, and with worse performance than Moscow claims.<cite>Less a "new generation of threats" than a stalled build with high paper potential and low delivery</cite></p>

<p>The real question isn't the boat's specifications on paper — it's whether Russian industry can build these boats in series, reliably, under wartime conditions and sanctions<a className="ref" href="#ref13">[13]</a>. If Russia ever delivers AIP (or the lithium-ion alternative) and moves to serial production, this picture will need revisiting; for now, Lada's actual contribution to the fleet's strike potential is minimal.</p>
